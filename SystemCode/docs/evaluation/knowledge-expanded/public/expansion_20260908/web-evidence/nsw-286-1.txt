L3@P0: Why do I need to clean and sanitise?
L4@P0: Effective cleaning and sanitising in your food business helps protect you and your customers against
L5@P0: the spread of bacteria and other organisms that cause foodborne illness. It also helps to reduce the
L6@P0: activity of pests in a food premises by eliminating food sources.
L7@P0: The Food Standards Code requires:
L8@P0: • a food premises, including all its equipment, to be maintained to an acceptable standard of
L9@P0: cleanliness, [Std. 3.2.2-19] and
L10@P0: • food contact equipment, such as kitchen benches, knives, chopping boards, pots, and meat slicers
L11@P0: etc., as well as eating and drinking utensils, to be in a clean and sanitary condition.
L12@P0: Difference between cleaning and sanitising
L13@P0: In the food industry, cleaning and sanitising is a two-step process. A surface needs to be thoroughly
L14@P0: cleaned before it is sanitised:
L15@P0: 1. Cleaning requires the use of warm to hot water, detergent and physical action to remove food
L16@P0: debris and dissolve grease and dirt to ensure the surfaces are clean to touch and free of visible
L17@P0: matter and odours.
L18@P0: 2. Sanitising is the process of applying heat (usually very hot water) or chemicals or a combination of
L19@P0: both heat and chemicals, to an already clean surface to reduce the number of bacteria and other
L20@P0: organisms to a safe level.
L21@P0: During the cleaning stage detergents ensure
L22@P0: food particles are broken down and
L23@P0: prevented from depositing back onto the
L24@P0: items being washed.
L25@P0: Effective cleaning is 90% of the overall
L26@P0: sanitation effort as cleaning removes most
L27@P0: of the bacteria present. The job of the
L28@P0: sanitiser is to kill the remaining bacteria
L29@P0: during the sanitising stage.
L30@P0: Cleaning and sanitising can be done
L31@P0: mechanically using dishwashers or manually
L32@P0-1: using wash up sinks and spray bottles. Figure 1. Benchtop cleaning.Cleaning and sanitising in retail food businesses

L34@P1: Cleaning and sanitising using dishwashers
L35@P1: All commercial dishwashers operate differently. High temperature
L36@P1: dishwashers sanitise using heat (hot water) while low temperature
L37@P1: dishwashers are complemented with chemical sanitisers. This is a very
L38@P1: technical process and food businesses should use the program that the
L39@P1: manufacturer has specified for sanitising. The manufacturer's instructions
L40@P1: should always be followed.
L41@P1: To sanitise with a dishwasher:
L42@P1: 1. make sure you have a suitable dishwasher that can clean and sanitise
L43@P1: quickly and effectively. Domestic dishwashers are generally not
L44@P1: suitable and are impractical for busy retail or hospitality food
L45@P1: businesses due to the very long cycles required to compensate for the
L46@P1: lower appliance temperatures
L47@P1: 2. use the correct type of detergent or sanitiser as outlined in the
L48@P1: manufacturer’s instructions
L49@P1: 3. use the hottest rinse cycle possible as per the manufacturer's
L50@P1: instructions
L51@P1: 4. look over equipment and utensils when removing them from the
L52@P1: dishwasher to check they are clean
L53@P1: 5. clean the dishwasher so there is no build-up of food residues
L54@P1: 6. regularly maintain and service the dishwasher according to the
L55@P1: manufacturer’s specifications.
L56@P1: Cleaning and sanitising using sinks or spray bottles
L57@P1: Clear and detailed instructions on how to correctly make up and use chemical sanitisers must be
L58@P1: either on the label of the sanitiser or provided by the supplier in a product information sheet.
L59@P1: Always make up and use chemical sanitisers by strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions.
L60@P1: There are many different types of sanitiser and each will vary greatly in how they should be used. If
L61@P1: clear instructions are not provided with the product, such as dilution rates and contact time, seek
L62@P1: advice from the supplier or manufacturer and source an alternative product if necessary.
L63@P1: Note: contact time means the amount of time a sanitiser must be in contact with an item, for it to work.
L64@P1: The six recommended steps for effective cleaning and sanitising using sinks are:
L65@P1: 1. Scrape or wipe away
L66@P1: food scraps
L67@P1: 2. Rinse with water
L68@P1: 3. Wash using warm to hot
L69@P1: water and detergent to
L70@P1: remove grease and dirt.
L71@P1: Soak if needed.
L72@P1: 4. Rinse off any loose dirt
L73@P1: or detergent residue
L74@P1: (sanitisers will not work
L75@P1: well in the presence of
L76@P1: food or detergent
L77@P1: residues). Figure 3. Cleaning and sanitising using a double bowl sink

L80@P2: 5. Sanitise with a chemical sanitiser:
L81@P2: • Make up the sanitising solution as per manufacturer’s instructions
L82@P2: • Dip equipment into the sanitising solution following manufacturer’s instructions making sure you
L83@P2: allow the appropriate contact time for the sanitiser to work
L84@P2: • For larger items that won’t fit in the sink use spray bottles to apply the sanitising solution to
L85@P2: equipment
L86@P2: • Wash off the sanitiser if necessary, as per manufacturer’s instructions
L87@P2: 6. Air dry or use single use towels.
L88@P2: Making up a sanitiser solution in a sink
L89@P2: To dilute your sanitiser of choice to the correct concentration in a sink, follow the steps below. This
L90@P2: calculation only needs to be done once for each sanitiser.
L91@P2: 1. Calculate the working volume of the sink by either:
L92@P2: a. Filling a container of known quantity (for example, a 10 litre bucket) with water, pouring it into a
L93@P2: sink the desired number of times and mark the sink at the right level.
L94@P2: Example: 6 times to make up 60 litres sink volume.
L95@P2: OR
L96@P2: b. Calculating the capacity of a square or rectangular sink by measuring the length, width and
L97@P2: depth of the sink (how high you fill the sink up to) in centimetres.
L98@P2: i. Multiply these three measurements to get the volume in cubic centimetres.
L99@P2: ii. Divide your answer by 1000 to determine the number of litres the sink will hold.
L100@P2: Example: a rectangular sink 40 cm wide, 50 cm long and filled to a height of 30 cm.
L101@P2: Figure 4. Measuring the sink to calculate volume.
L102@P2: Example:
L103@P2: 40 x 50 x 30
L104@P2: = 60,000cm3
L105@P2: 60,000 / 1000
L106@P2: = 60 litres

L109@P3: 2. Calculate how much sanitiser to add to your sink
L110@P3: a. Check the dilution rate on the sanitiser label or in
L111@P3: the product information sheet for the required
L112@P3: purpose, for example, for use as a no rinse
L113@P3: sanitiser.
L114@P3: b. The dilution rate is the amount of sanitiser to water.
L115@P3: Example: 1:250 means 1 part of sanitiser to 250
L116@P3: parts of water or 1 ml sanitiser to 250 ml water.
L117@P3: 3. Divide the volume of the sink (as calculated in step 1)
L118@P3: by the sanitiser dilution rate. Mix the sanitiser to the
L119@P3: water in the sink.
L120@P3: Note: if you change sanitisers or vary the volume of water in the sink the calculation will not be correct
L121@P3: and your sanitising step may be ineffective. In this case you will need to recalculate the correct dilution
L122@P3: as above.
L123@P3: Diluting a sanitiser to the correct concentration is critical in preventing the spread of harmful bacteria
L124@P3: that cause foodborne illness.
L125@P3: Businesses using store-bought bleach as a sanitiser should refer to the dilution instructions outlined in
L126@P3: Chemical sanitisers: advice for food businesses (PDF, 231KB).
