L694@P23: 1.6 Serving raw or undercooked foods – infectious bacteria, parasites and viruses
L695@P23: The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention nominate a number of simple precautions that consumers can
L696@P23: use to protect themselves from foodborne diseases (CDC, 2010). The first of these is:
L697@P23: Cook meat, poultry and eggs thoroughly. Using a thermometer to measure the internal temperature of meat is a
L698@P23: good way to be sure that it is cooked sufficiently to kill bacteria.
L699@P23: Many other food agencies make very similar recommendations including the NSW Food Authority (NSWFA, 2011c).
L700@P23: Despite the advice, people still choose to eat a wide variety of raw or undercooked animal products. For example:
L701@P23: Carpaccio Yukhoe Rare hamburger Egg nog
L702@P23: Ceviche Steak tartare Sashimi Kibbeh
L703@P23: Egg butter Tiramisu Raw Milk Cheese
L704@P23: The risk with these foods is related to the potential and, in some cases, likely presence of pathogenic bacteria,
L705@P23: parasites or viruses. The risks can be illustrated by:
L706@P23: • disease prevalence statistics:
L707@P23: - Vibrio parahaemolyticus infection accounted for 45–60% of food related infections in Japan (Berger, 2011).
L708@P23: This organism is associated with seafood and the illness is related to the high consumption of raw seafood
L709@P23: in Japan.
L710@P23: - In France, more than 200 cases of hepatitis E are reported each year. It has been shown in developed
L711@P23: industrialised countries that pigs are reservoirs of the virus. Acute hepatitis E has been linked to persons
L712@P23: who consumed uncooked pig liver sausages. These sausages are good candidates for the transmission of
L713@P23: hepatitis E because they are made with pig liver, are not cooked during the manufacturing process, and are
L714@P23: often eaten uncooked (ProMed Mail, 2011).
L715@P23: - In April 2014, the first reported HEV outbreak was reported in Australia. Analytical evidence linked the
L716@P23: outbreak to consumption of pork liver pâté (Communicable Diseases Branch, 2015).
L717@P23: • outbreak reports:
L718@P23: - In April and May 2011, there was a food poisoning outbreak in Japan caused by enterohemorrhagic E. coli
L719@P23: O111:H8 and O157:H7 from raw beef dishes at branches of a barbeque restaurant. The outbreak affected
L720@P23: 181 people and five people died (Watahiki et al., 2014).
L721@P23: - Nine cases of paragonimiasis (a parasitic infection) were caused by the consumption of raw or undercooked
L722@P23: crayfish in Missouri (CDC, 2010).
L723@P23: - In 2010, a Salmonella outbreak in Albury, Australia affected 179 people. Home-made aioli, a garlic
L724@P23: mayonnaise that includes raw egg, was the cause (NSWFA, 2011b).
L725@P23: • other sources:
L726@P23: - In recent decades, the custom of eating foods raw, or only partially cooked, has grown worldwide and has
L727@P23: led to the emergence of parasitic diseases in ethnic groups where eating raw or undercooked meat was not
L728@P23: previously common (Macpherson, 2005).
L729@P23: - Three large studies in Europe have pinpointed uncooked meat as the most important risk factor for
L730@P23-24: Toxoplasma gondii infection in pregnant women. Toxoplasmosis is generally considered a serious health 25
L731@P24: problem in pregnant women, who can pass the infection to the foetus or newborn, and in people who are
L732@P24: immunocompromised (Kijlstra & Jongert, 2008).
L733@P24: - The tradition of eating raw fish is becoming increasingly fashionable in many countries. This had led to a
L734@P24: dramatic rise in the incidence of fish borne parasitic infections in previously unaffected ethnic groups
L735@P24: (Macpherson, 2005).
L736@P24: - All wild caught seawater and freshwater fish must be considered at risk of containing viable parasites of
L737@P24: human health concern if these products are to be eaten raw or almost raw (EFSA, 2010).
L738@P24: - A range of traditional Japanese dishes utilise the addition of raw egg, for examples tsukimi udon/soba,
L739@P24: sukiyaki, tamago kake gohan, oyakodon and natto. Eggs have caused a number of food poisoning cases in
L740@P24: Japan since 1989 (Bureau of Social Welfare and Public Health, Tokyo Metropolitan Government, n.d.).
L741@P24: Some sous vide recipes call for low temperature short time (LT ST) cooking and in some cases the final food remains
L742@P24: raw or undercooked. Such foods have a higher risk of causing bacterial, viral or parasitic illness.
L743@P24: Hands-on: safer alternatives – raw and undercooked animal products
L744@P24: Businesses must be aware that meat, seafood and eggs that have not been cooked present an increased level of food
L745@P24: safety risk to consumers. These products should not be used for cook chill food service.
L746@P24: Vulnerable people such as children, the elderly, immunocompromised people or those with a serious illness are at
L747@P24: greater risk of suffering from the more severe consequences of foodborne illness. Notifying these members of the
L748@P24: vulnerable population can be done by written warnings on menus, posting signs and verbal disclosure by staff.

L750@P24: Hazard Control measures
L751@P24: Seafood:
L752@P24: Parasites
L753@P24: • Purchase safer fish: marine fish are safer than freshwater fish; farmed (pellet feed) fish are
L754@P24: safer than wild caught fish; higher value marine fish (tuna, Atlantic salmon) are safer than
L755@P24: lower value marine fish (mackerel, squid)8.
L756@P24: • Carefully examine fish during cleaning and filleting. Parasites are small but many can be seen
L757@P24: with the naked eye or when fillets are ‘candled’ (a strong light is shone through the fillet).
L758@P24: • Use the freshest, best quality fish that is inspected during processing.
L759@P24: • Freeze fish prior to preparation. For fish to be consumed without adequate cooking, the
L760@P24: European Food Safety Agency (EFSA, 2010) requires the following:
L761@P24: - Freeze at -15°C for at least 96 hours, or
L762@P24: - Freeze at -20°C for at least 24 hours, or
L763@P24: - Freeze at -35°C for at least 15 hours.
L764@P24: • Cook all parts of the fish to 60°C for 1 minute (EFSA, 2010). This mild cooking process will be
L765@P24: compatible with many fish recipes.

L767@P24: Information on parasites that impact the safety of seafood is provided in the Risk Assessment of the Seafood Safety
L768@P24-25: Scheme on the Food Authority’s website26
L769@P25: Hazard Control measures
L770@P25: Seafood:
L771@P25: Bacteria
L772@P25: • The main bacterial pathogen of marine origin in Australia (Vibrio parahaemolyticus) dies when
L773@P25: exposed to refrigeration temperatures (ICMSF, 1996a) and is inactivated by fairly mild cooking
L774@P25: temperatures (van Asselt & Zwietering, 2006). However, fish contaminated with bacteria from
L775@P25: the kitchen environment or food handlers might not be made safe after mild cooking
L776@P25: processes.
L777@P25: • Do not cook minced or reformed seafood (including fish cakes and products formed using
L778@P25: transglutaminase or other ‘meat glues’) using low temperature short time (LT ST) processes.
L779@P25: • The ‘parasite cook’ recommended by EFSA will provide control of V. parahaemolyticus.
L780@P25: Meat:
L781@P25: Parasites
L782@P25: • Purchase through reputable supply chains. Meat inspection prevents meat infected with many,
L783@P25: but not all, parasites from being marketed.
L784@P25: • Freeze meat prior to preparation. The EFSA fish freezing requirements above will provide
L785@P25: useful control.
L786@P25: Meat: Bacteria • Do not cook mechanically tenderised, minced, pumped, rolled or reformed meats (including
L787@P25: meats prepared using ‘meat glue’) using low temperature short time (LT ST) processes.
L788@P25: • Avoid very low temperature cooking. Water bath temperatures above 55°C and, more
L789@P25: generally, meat surface temperatures above 54.5°C will provide a measure of decontamination
L790@P25: as cooking proceeds.
L791@P25: • Sous vide chefs should test recipes to see if slightly higher temperatures can be used. Figure 2
L792@P25: shows relative thermal inactivation of Salmonella, generated by the ComBase predictive
L793@P25: microbiology modelling website. The yellow curve shows some reduction at 54.5°C with
L794@P25: increasing lethality at 55°C (pink), 55.5°C (orange) and 56°C (blue). A small increase in
L795@P25: temperature above 54.5°C will provide markedly increased destruction of surface bacteria. As
L796@P25: noted above, bacteria are typically found on the surface of meat providing it has not been
L797@P25: tenderised, minced, pumped, rolled, reformed or ‘glued’.
L798@P25: Eggs: Bacteria • Sous vide recipes that result in fully cooked eggs are available and their use is recommended9
L799@P25: .
L800@P25: Strict time/temp for fully cooked eggs should be observed as undercooked sous vide eggs
L801@P25: were one of the implicated sources of a Canadian foodborne illnesses reported in 2014.

L809@P26: Additional notes:
L810@P26: • Equipment (portable circulators in particular). The accuracy of many models is hard to determine, making
L811@P26: temperature management a concern. Monitoring and maintenance of equipment is extremely important as
L812@P26: temperature is a key indicator that the food will be safe to consume at the end of the process.
L813@P26: • All equipment used for sous vide needs must undergo thorough cleaning and sanitising at regular intervals and
L814@P26: the process must be documented. This should be reflected in the food safety plan for sous vide cooking. Lack of
L815@P26: cleaning and sanitising may lead to problems such as biofilm build up.
L816@P26: • Appropriate food-grade bags must be used. Vacuum sealed bags may leak if heated for a very long time. The
L817@P26: integrity of the bag may also be compromised if inferior quality bags are used.
L818@P26: • It is important to take temperatures precisely when verifying sous vide protocols. Necessary equipment includes
L819@P26: a calibrated thermometer (to 0.1 degrees), cell foam tape and a document to record temperatures. Internal
L820@P26: digital probe tip thermometers must be used to monitor temperatures. Thermometers should be calibrated,
L821@P26-27: traceable and certified (BCCDC, 2017).28
L822@P27: Conclusion
L823@P27: Sous vide seems like a new food service technology but it has a history spanning five decades in France and three
L824@P27: decades in other countries. It has been found in many markets including extended shelf life ready meals. Today it is
L825@P27: used by catering companies, restaurants and, increasingly, home cooks.
L826@P27: There are risks with the sous vide process that must be managed. Leading sous vide chefs are aware of these risks
L827@P27: and food safety has been a prominent focus in their kitchens and recipe books. As far as can be determined from the
L828@P27: scientific literature and foodborne illness databases, sous vide chefs have been successful in managing food safety
L829@P27: and food poisoning attributed to sous vide has rarely been identified.
L830@P27: The risks associated with sous vide foods can be reduced if:
L831@P27: • thinner portions of food are prepared so that heating and cooling are rapid.
L832@P27: • products are not placed in the water bath tightly on top of each other. Overcrowding affects cooking times and
L833@P27: increases the chance of cold spots occurring.
L834@P27: • water bath temperatures of at least 55°C are used so that the growth of Clostridium perfringens is first
L835@P27: prevented and then destruction of the cells commences.
L836@P27: • the time food is held at temperatures below 54.5°C during cooking is limited to 6 hours.
L837@P27: • professional equipment with adequate heating capacity and excellent temperature control is used.
L838@P27: • water and/or food temperatures are checked using a tip sensitive digital thermometer that is accurate to 0.1°C.
L839@P27: • prepared foods are not stored for extended times unless processes have been validated.
L840@P27: • risks are not compounded. Cooking large portions of mechanically tenderised meat for extended times at low
L841@P27: temperatures would be irresponsible.
L842@P27: If you choose to include on your menu foods that remain essentially raw, you must make clear the health implications
L843@P27: the food could pose to the consumer.
L844@P27: New practitioners of sous vide must be aware of the food safety risks and avoid overly experimental applications of the
L845@P27-28: technology.29

L946@P31: Appendix – Variation to the 2-hour/4-hour rule
L947@P31: Information contained in this document is consistent with the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code and its
L948@P31: associated guidance materials. However, section 1.1 includes some cooking times that are inconsistent with the ‘2-
L949@P31: hour/4-hour rule’. The tables were developed and published by Baldwin (2011).
L950@P31: Baldwin is very widely quoted in food enthusiast circles. Some of his sous vide work is published in peer reviewed
L951@P31: journals and his self-published material is submitted to food scientists prior to publication. Baldwin wrote his Sous Vide
L952@P31: cook book while a PhD student in mathematics. He is a sous vide enthusiast and has turned his mathematical
L953@P31: prowess to establishing cooking times for sous vide foods. Food safety is prominent in his book and web-based
L954@P31: guidance material.
L955@P31: Baldwin only tabulated times that comply with the U.S. Food Code 4 hour limit for foods that are served unpasteurised.
L956@P31: However, for foods that are to be pasteurised he allows up to 6 hours to reach 54.4°C. This limit is based on data
L957@P31: presented in a paper by Willardsen et al. (1978) and is designed to limit the growth of C. perfringens prior to
L958@P31: pasteurisation. That means that cooking cycles for thicker cuts are limited to higher temperature water baths so that
L959@P31: internal temperatures reach target within 6 hours.
