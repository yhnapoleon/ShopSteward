L294@P10: Food safety hazards
L295@P10: 1.1 Low temperature cooking – bacterial growth
L296@P10: Scientific literature on bacterial growth in low temperature cooking processes is quite limited. The Food Safety and
L297@P10: Inspection Service of the United States Department of Agriculture (FSIS, 1999) mentions the risk of ‘slow come up
L298@P10: time’ for certain meat and poultry products. The guideline states that ‘dwell times of greater than 6 hours in the 50°F
L299@P10: (10°C) to 130°F (54.4°C) range should be viewed as especially hazardous, as this temperature range can foster the
L300@P10: growth of many pathogens of concern’. Food can dwell in this temperature range due to ‘slow come up time’, or by
L301@P10: being held for an inordinate amount of time in that range. The guideline notes that the multiplication of pathogens can
L302@P10: be so great then even recooking may be ineffective in making the product safe. Toxigenic bacteria can release toxins
L303@P10: into the products. Some of the toxins, such as those of Staphylococcus aureus, are extremely heat stable and are not
L304@P10: inactivated by normal recooking temperatures.
L305@P10: The Food Code (US Food and Drug Administration, 2009) includes several clauses on time as a public health control.
L306@P10: Clause 3–501.19 (1) (B), is similar in intent to the ‘2-hour/4-hour rule’ for temperature control outlined in Food
L307@P10: Standards Australia New Zealand website (FSANZ, n.d). The US Food Code allows for a maximum of four hours
L308@P10: between 5°C and 57°C, whereas the comparable Australian guideline temperatures are 5°C and 60°C.
L309@P10: Low temperature long time (LT LT) cooking is not new. Braising and crockpot are familiar examples used by some
L310@P10: steak houses to prepare prime roasts. The main benefit is tenderisation of cheaper meat cuts. Care must be taken if
L311@P10: unusually low temperatures are used to achieve an undercooked or rare product.
L312@P10: The main food safety risks of LT LT cooking are Clostridium perfringens and bacteria that form potentially hazardous
L313@P10: heat stable toxins in food such as Bacillus cereus and S. aureus. C. perfringens appears to be the pathogen best
L314@P10: adapted to growth during processing of sous vide meats (Willardsen et al, 1978) and if this organism is effectively
L315@P10: controlled, the risks from the other bacteria will be minimal.
L316@P10: The emergence of LT LT cooking can result in some foods being held at temperatures for periods of time that do not
L317@P10: comply with Australian or US guidelines. For example, mathematician and published author on sous vide, Douglas
L318@P10: Baldwin (2011, 2012, 2022), includes some recipes (tables of cooking times) where the combination of temperature
L319@P10-11: and time is outside the guidelines.12
L320@P11: Baldwin only tabulates times that comply with the US Food Code four hour limit for foods that are served
L321@P11: unpasteurised. However, for foods that are pasteurised during cooking, he allows up to six hours to reach 54.5°C. This
L322@P11: combination of time and temperature challenges two familiar concepts—the ‘temperature danger zone’ and the ‘2-
L323@P11: hour/4-hour rule’.
L324@P11: However, based on the comments of The Food Safety and Inspection Service of the United States Department of
L325@P11: Agriculture (FSIS, 1999) and the studies by Willardsen et al (1978), the NSW Food Authority accepts that any risk to
L326@P11: food safety is low.

L443@P15: 1.2 Product cooling – bacterial growth
L444@P15: The potential for bacterial growth during cooling and reheating of sous vide foods is no different from conventional
L445@P15: processes. The Food Standards Code addresses the issue and guidance is available in Safe Food Australia (2016).
L446@P15: C. perfringens is the major cause of foodborne illness associated with inadequate cooling and reheating of foods. This
L447@P15: pathogen has important characteristics that result in it being a regular cause of foodborne illness:
L448@P15: • It forms a tough heat resistant spore that is not killed during normal cooking. Under suitable conditions the spore
L449@P15: will germinate to produce actively growing bacteria.
L450@P15: • It grows in the absence of oxygen and food packed under vacuum in a pouch provides an ideal environment for
L451@P15: growth.
L452@P15: • The bacteria can grow exceptionally quickly in warm food, especially meat. They will double in number every ten
L453@P15: minutes at optimal temperatures and grow quickly in the range of 30 to 50°C.
L454@P15: • When temperature-abused food is consumed, the bacteria will form spores in the gut and, in the process,
L455@P15: release a toxin. If large numbers of the bacteria were present in the food, the toxin will result in food poisoning
L456@P15: with profuse diarrhoea and severe cramping pain.

L500@P17: 1.3 Extended refrigerated storage of food – botulism
L501@P17: Botulism is a term used to describe an intoxication caused by C. botulinum neurotoxins. From a food safety point of
L502@P17: view C. botulinum remains one of the most important foodborne bacteria. It is characterised by its:
L503@P17: • growth in the absence of air,
L504@P17: • production of a tough spore that survives normal cooking,
L505@P17: • growth across a wide temperature range, including some strains that grow at refrigeration temperatures, and
L506@P17: • production of the most potent group of biological neurotoxins known (Szabo and Gibson, 2003).
L507@P17: A product stored in a sealed pouch, prepared using a delicate cooking cycle and stored refrigerated for lengthy
L508@P17: periods, presents an environment where non-proteolytic (cold-tolerant) strains of C. botulinum are an undoubted
L509@P17: hazard. Most of the food safety concerns about these types of products have been focussed on the potential for
L510@P17: growth and toxin production by these strains.
L511@P17: In 2008, the UK Food Standards Agency (UKFSA) provided guidance on the safety and shelf life of vacuum packed
L512@P17: and modified atmosphere packed chilled foods with respect to non-proteolytic C. botulinum. The UKFSA summarised
L513@P17: the recommendations of their Advisory Committee on the Microbiological Safety of Food (ACMSF, 1992, 2007) on
L514@P17: vacuum packaging and associated processes. The ACMSF (2007) recommended a maximum of ten day shelf life for
L515@P17: vacuum and modified atmosphere foods stored between 3°C and 8°C when other controlling factors could not be
L516@P17: identified. The report noted that at less than 3°C the growth of non-proteolytic C. botulinum does not occur and that
L517@P17: foods stored at less than 3°C are outside the scope of the guidance.
L518@P17: The ACMSF recommended that, in addition to chill temperatures which should be maintained throughout the food
L519@P17: chain, the following control factors should be used singly or in combination to prevent growth and toxin production by
L520@P17: non-proteolytic C. botulinum in chilled foods with a shelf life of more than ten days:
L521@P17: • A heat treatment of 90°C for 10 minutes or equivalent lethality.
L522@P17: • A pH of 5 or less throughout the food and throughout all components of complex foods.
L523@P17: • A minimum salt level of 3.5% in the aqueous phase throughout the food and throughout all components of
L524@P17: complex foods.
L525@P17: • A water activity of 0.97 or less throughout the food and throughout all components of complex foods.
L526@P17: • A combination of heat and preservative factors which can be shown consistently to prevent growth and toxin
L527@P17: production by cold tolerant C. botulinum.

L529@P18: 1.4 Extended refrigerated storage of food – listeriosis
L530@P18: Foodborne listeriosis is a relatively rare but serious disease with high fatality rates (20–30%) compared with other
L531@P18: foodborne microbial pathogens, such as Salmonella. The disease largely affects specific segments of the population
L532@P18: who have increased susceptibilities.
L533@P18: L. monocytogenes is an opportunistic pathogen that most often affects:
L534@P18: • people with a severe underlying disease or condition, e.g. immunosuppression, HIV/AIDS and chronic
L535@P18: conditions such as cirrhosis that impair the immune system,
L536@P18: • pregnant women,
L537@P18: • unborn or newly delivered infants, and
L538@P18: • the elderly.
L539@P18: L. monocytogenes is widely dispersed in the environment and foods. An important factor in foodborne listeriosis is that
L540@P18: the pathogen can grow to significant numbers at refrigeration temperatures when given sufficient time. Although
L541@P18: listeriosis is a relatively rare disease, the severity of the disease and the very frequent involvement of industrially
L542@P18: processed foods, especially during outbreaks, mean that the social and economic impact of listeriosis is among the
L543@P18: highest of the foodborne diseases (WHO, 2004).
L544@P18: Cox and Bauler (2008) provide the following guidance on control of L. monocytogenes in cook chill foods:
L545@P18: • Because of its sensitivity to heat, cooking will reduce the number of L. monocytogenes by 1,000,000-fold
L546@P18: provided all parts of the food reach a temperature of, or equivalent to, 75°C. This is known as a 6D process.
L547@P18: • Although growth does not stop, storage at 5°C or below will substantially slow the growth of L. monocytogenes
L548@P18: in cook chill foods.
L549@P18: • Limiting the storage time and/or shelf life are other major controls that can be used.
L550@P18: Hands-on: refrigerated storage of sous vide foods
L551@P18: Modern culinary sous vide is less about extended storage and more about taste. For example, Thomas Keller’s book,
L552@P18: Under Pressure (2008), does not encourage storage for extended periods unless the food is frozen. The book focuses
L553@P18: on the gastronomic benefits of cooking under vacuum at very controlled temperatures. Food safety is accentuated upfront and if others follow his storage advice C. botulinum will not be a risk. Baldwin (2022) provides sound advice on
L554@P18: storage temperatures with only brief storage periods recommended at temperatures above 3.3°C.
L555@P18: The NSW Food Authority’s default guidance for sous vide food and certain other products in sealed packages which
L556@P18: are protected from contamination after thermal processing, is below:
L557@P18: • With a 90°C/10 minute equivalent cook and storage at 5°C – shelf life as validated.
L558@P18: • With a 70°C/2 minute equivalent cook and storage at less than 3°C – shelf life as validated.
L559@P18: • With a 70°C/2 minute equivalent cook and storage at 5°C – a 10 day shelf life.
L560@P18: The process controls and support programs required to validate an extended shelf life are generally not plausible in a
L561@P18: busy restaurant kitchen. Restaurants should limit refrigerated storage of pasteurised sous vide foods to ten days.
L562@P18: Cooked but not pasteurised foods should only be held refrigerated for brief periods. Kennedy (2004) refers to ‘short
L563@P18: term cook chill’ and notes that it is often called ‘5-day cook chill’, as that is a common time period used by mass
L564@P18-19: caterers. Five days at 5°C is the recommended upper limit for storage of cooked but not pasteurised sous vide food.20
L565@P19: 1.5 Product reheating – bacterial growth
L566@P19: The potential for bacterial growth during cooling and reheating of sous vide foods is no different to conventional
L567@P19: processes, and the guidance documents mentioned in section 1.2 are relevant.
L568@P19: C. perfringens is commonly encountered in food service and home catering settings. Often large portions of food and
L569@P19: more than one failure to handle food safely are involved. Examples include the following:
L570@P19: • A restaurant cooks a casserole, curry or chilli in a large stock pot, cools it for two hours on the kitchen bench
L571@P19: and refrigerates it overnight. The food is ladled into a tray in the hot display to reheat and then served at lunch
L572@P19: time. The food at the centre of the stock pot never cooled and slowly reheated to only a low temperature while
L573@P19: on display. C. perfringens grew to very large numbers and many customers developed food poisoning.
L574@P19: • A large roast, e.g. a turkey, was cooked the day before a large family function. It was cooled on the bench for
L575@P19: several hours, wrapped in aluminium foil and then refrigerated. It was reheated briefly in an overworked oven
L576@P19: prior to service. The food was not properly cooled or reheated. C. perfringens grew to large numbers and
L577@P19: caused illness in many that attended the function.
L578@P19: Small portions of sous vide food are relatively easy to cool and reheat. With attention to detail, the risk of
L579@P19: C. perfringens in small pouches of sous vide food can be controlled.
L580@P19: Hands-on: sous vide reheating

L582@P19: useful guidance on reheating of foods. Table 5, which derived from the guide in his website, shows approximate
