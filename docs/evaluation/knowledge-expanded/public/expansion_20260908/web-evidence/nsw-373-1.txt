L1@P0: Guidelines for the use of non-potable water in
L2@P0: food businesses
L3@P0: These guidelines are designed to help food businesses ensure water used for food preparation and
L4@P0: human consumption is safe in the event of a microbiologically unsafe water supply.
L5@P0: A water supply may not be safe due to inadequate treatment or an interruption to treatment.
L6@P0: Concerns and issues about the chemical safety of the water supply should be raised with the
L7@P0: environmental health officers from your local council or Public Health Unit.
L8@P0: Potable water in food businesses
L9@P0: Food laws require food businesses to use ‘potable water’ for all activities that use water on the
L10@P0: premises, unless the use of ‘non-potable water’ will not affect the safety of the food.
L11@P0: Potable water means it is acceptable and safe for human consumption, and must be used in a food
L12@P0: business for:
L13@P0: • washing food and/or food ingredients
L14@P0: • cooking
L15@P0: • adding to food and drinks
L16@P0: • making ice
L17@P0: • cleaning food contact surfaces
L18@P0: • cleaning food containers and utensils
L19@P0: • personal hygiene.
L20@P0: Non-potable water
L21@P0: Non-potable water can be used only when it will not affect food safety, such as the flushing of toilets
L22@P0: and cleaning non-food contact surfaces such as floors, or if it is treated to be safe for human
L23@P0: consumption. If in doubt, please consult the environmental health officers from your local council or
L24@P0: Public Health Unit.
L25@P0: A water supply is not guaranteed to be safe when:
L26@P0: a. the reticulated drinking water supply to a food business has a temporary interruption to the
L27@P0: treatment of that supply, maybe because of water treatment plant breakdown, fire or flooding
L28@P0: b. a reticulated water supply does not meet drinking water standards
L29@P0: c. it is a private supply, such as:
L30@P0: • rainwater tanks
L31@P0: • groundwater, such as bores
L32@P0-1: • surface water sources, such as a river or lake.Guidelines for the use of non-potable water in food businesses

L34@P1: Food laws and potable water
L35@P1: Standard 3.2.3 clause 4 of the Australian New Zealand Food Standards Code (the Code) requires a
L36@P1: food business to use potable water for all activities that use water on the food premises, unless the
L37@P1: business demonstrates the use of non-potable water will not adversely affect the safety of the food
L38@P1: handled by the food business.
L39@P1: In addition, Standard 3.2.3, clause 14 (2) of the Code requires hand washing facilities to be connected
L40@P1: to, or otherwise provided with, a supply of warm, running potable water.

L107@P2: Possible health risks from non-potable water
L108@P2: Microbiological contamination
L109@P2: Contaminated water may contain harmful microorganisms, such as viruses, bacteria such as
L110@P2: Salmonella, Campylobacter or E. coli, and gastro-intestinal parasites such as Giardia or
L111@P2: Cryptosporidium. These harmful microorganisms, known as pathogens, are invisible to the naked eye
L112@P2: and may be present in clear water.
L113@P2: Drinking water containing these microorganisms can cause severe gastroenteritis, possibly lasting for
L114@P2: several weeks. Infants, the elderly and people with suppressed immune systems are more likely to be
L115@P2: affected.
L116@P2: Chemical contamination
L117@P2: Chemical contaminants are less common than microbiological contaminants but can still be present in
L118@P2: the environment.
L119@P2: Soil from old industrial, mining or agricultural areas may contain arsenic, heavy metals, pesticide
L120@P2: residues or other chemicals. If dust is blown onto roofs and is washed into rainwater tanks, chemical
L121@P2: residues may build up in the water. Runoff from roofs in urban or industrial areas may also contain
L122@P2: chemical pollutants from the air.
L123@P2: Lead-based paints or flashing used on some older roofs may also flake off and be washed into
L124@P2: rainwater tanks. Tar-based coatings can bind other harmful organic chemicals such as pesticides to
L125@P2: the roof surface and also make it difficult to clean.
L126@P2: Harmful smoky residues from solid wood heaters can also condense near flues on roofs. These
L127@P2: chemicals may leach from the roof surface over time and may be washed into rainwater tanks.
L128@P2: Aerial application of fertilisers and pesticides such as 'crop dusting' can sometimes result in these
L129@P2: agricultural chemicals entering rainwater tanks. Agricultural chemicals may also drift or be washed into
L130@P2: drains, irrigation channels, local streams and dams.
L131@P2: The below treatment methods outline how to make the water supply safe if there is the possibility that
L132@P2: the water supply is microbiologically unsafe. If you suspect that your water supply could be chemically
L133@P2: unsafe, consult with your local council or Public Health Unit.
L134@P2: Whatever the water source, it is the responsibility of the food business to check what possible
L135@P2-3: chemical contaminants can get into the water supply.Guidelines for the use of non-potable water in food businesses

L137@P3: Water treatment methods
L138@P3: Temporary treatment
L139@P3: There can be times when a water supplier may notify that its normally potable water supply will either
L140@P3: be shut off for maintenance or has been temporarily contaminated, which is usually microbiological but
L141@P3: may be accompanied by dirty water. They may advise to boiI the water for a short time or to treat
L142@P3: possible contamination in the water supply.
L143@P3: There are things a food business can do to ensure the safety of their water supply during a short-term
L144@P3: interruption.
L145@P3: Table 1 Temporary treatment
L146@P3: Water used for Action or treatment
L147@P3: Drinking Use either commercially bottled water or water that has been brought to a
L148@P3: rolling boil, that is, the water is boiling vigorously for at least one (1)
L149@P3: minute.
L150@P3: Ice making If you need to make ice during this time, only make it from water that is
L151@P3: suitable for drinking.
L152@P3: Washing hands Use a water container with a tap that contains either bottled water or
L153@P3: water brought to a rolling boil for at least one (1) minute, or that has been
L154@P3: disinfected with chlorine (see chlorination guide below).
L155@P3: Cleaning equipment For cleaning equipment, such as pots, pans, cutting boards, dishes and
L156@P3: cutlery, the following options can be considered:
L157@P3: • sanitise by immersing the utensils in hot water at or hotter than 77°C
L158@P3: for at least 30 seconds. As it is unlikely a hot water system will deliver
L159@P3: water at a minimum of 77°C at the sink, it may be necessary to use a
L160@P3: water heater in the sink to maintain the temperature to at least 77°C
L161@P3: or an urn to feed water to the sink. A rinsing basket to submerge the
L162@P3: utensils in the water is desirable for safety reasons
L163@P3: • use a commercial dishwasher capable of sanitising
L164@P3: • use disposable cups, plates and other utensils
L165@P3: • use a chemical sanitiser for cleaning eating and drinking utensils and
L166@P3: for other utensils that require sanitising, such as large mixing bowls
L167@P3: and chopping boards that will not fit in the commercial dishwasher.
L168@P3: Cleaning floors and
L169@P3: non-food contact
L170@P3: surfaces
L171@P3: If you want to sanitise these surfaces, washing in water with a
L172@P3: concentration of chlorine 100-200 mg/L is adequate.
L173@P3: Note: In sewered areas, the responsible authority should be consulted as to whether
L174@P3: discharge of effluent containing these concentrations of chlorine is permitted. Also, care
L175@P3: should be taken if you have a septic tank as this may compromise the septic system.
L176@P3-4: Please consult your local council or Public Health Unit.Guidelines for the use of non-potable water in food businesses

L178@P4: Permanent treatment
L179@P4: Food businesses can either follow the advice in the previous section on a more permanent basis or
L180@P4: seek a longer-term solution to managing the safety of their water supply.
L181@P4: Treating water in a water storage vessel – such as a water tank
L182@P4: If the water source is from a non-potable water supply, groundwater or surface water, a practical way
L183@P4: to ensure the water supply is safe is to periodically fill a water storage vessel from the water supply
L184@P4: and treat the water in the vessel with chlorine.
L185@P4: Storage vessels for treated water should:
L186@P4: • be clean
L187@P4: • have covers
L188@P4: • be above ground level
L189@P4: • be in a cool position
L190@P4: • be cleaned periodically
L191@P4: • be mosquito proof
L192@P4: • be unlikely to taint the water (seek advice
L193@P4: from manufacturer).
L194@P4: Chlorination guide
L195@P4: • The initial dose should give a free chlorine residual of 5.0 mg/L.
L196@P4: • There should be enough chlorine to give a free chlorine residual of 1.0 mg/L after 30 minutes
L197@P4: contact time.
L198@P4: • Check after 30 minutes using a colour comparator, like the ones used for swimming pools.
L199@P4: • If necessary, add more chlorine to ensure that the required minimum of 1.0 mg/L maintained for 30
L200@P4: minutes, is achieved.
L201@P4: • Each time the storage vessel is running low, refill it and re-treat with chlorine.
L202@P4: • Chlorinate each time the storage vessel is filled with water from a non-potable source.
L203@P4: • Tables 2, 3, and 4 outline how much available chlorine is required to achieve the free chlorine
L204@P4: residual target.
L205@P4: • Some household bleach contains alkalis and other chemicals such as perfumes. The alkalis in
L206@P4: these products will increase the pH of the water, often above pH 9 which is not satisfactory. Make
L207@P4: sure to source a suitable chlorine product.

L235@P5: Alternative permanent water sterilisation methods
L236@P5: There are many methods that can be used to treat the water supply for microbiological safety. Two
L237@P5: such treatment methods that are chemical free are ultraviolet sterilisation and ozone treatment.
L238@P5: Search online for 'water treatment or equipment' for alternative water sterilisation methods.
L239@P5: If you have any doubts as to what to do, employ the services of a water treatment expert to advise the
L240@P5: best solution to meet your business needs, or contact your local council or Public Health Unit.

L243@P5: Ultraviolet (UV)
L244@P5: steriliser
L245@P5: UV sterilisers can be installed at point of entry of water to the business to
L246@P5: destroy pathogenic organisms.
L247@P5: UV disinfection is less effective in dirty or cloudy water as the light cannot
L248@P5: penetrate the water. Filtration may be necessary to remove suspended
L249@P5: particles before UV disinfection.
L250@P5: Ozone treatment Uses ozone gas to treat the water. Consult a water treatment expert for
L251@P5: advice.

L254@P6: Regular microbiological and chemical testing
L255@P6: Verify that the water treatment methods are working by arranging periodic samples for microbiological
L256@P6: analysis.
L257@P6: Chemical analysis of the water is also advisable, particularly when a new bore is used or to test
L258@P6: rainwater tanks following bushfires. Consult your local council or Public Health Unit about what to
L259@P6: sample and how often.
L260@P6: Water testing
L261@P6: Many analytical laboratories in NSW can advise on chemical, microbiological and algal testing of
L262@P6: water. Search for ‘water testing’ online to find a local laboratory.
