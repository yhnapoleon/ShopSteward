L17@P2: Executive summary
L18@P2: Date marking of foods is regulated by Standard 1.2.5 – Date Marking of Food of the Australia New Zealand Food
L19@P2: Standards Code.
L20@P2: If:
L21@P2: • nutrient levels decline to unacceptable levels in a food intended by the manufacturer to form the sole source of
L22@P2: nutrition for a person’s diet for a specified period or
L23@P2: • the food can become microbiologically unsafe before the food noticeably spoils
L24@P2: then a use by date is applicable.
L25@P2: Where deterioration of a food affects consumer acceptance without impacting health and safety a best before date
L26@P2: is applicable. Foods with a shelf life of at least two years are not required to display a best before date.
L27@P2: The length of the use by time for foods can be determined by using storage trials to estimate the physical, chemical
L28@P2: and microbiological stability of the food. Interpretation of results requires technical experience and competence.
L29@P2: Some products will require additional studies such as computer models of microbial growth or challenge studies.
L30@P2: It is the manufacturer’s responsibility to determine shelf life. Reliable use by or best before times cannot be
L31@P2-3: determined by guesswork or by copying the shelf life of a competitor’s product. 4

L33@P3: When is date marking required?
L34@P3: Date marking of foods is regulated by Standard 1.2.5. A fact sheet (FSANZ 2003) and a user guide (ANZFA 2001)
L35@P3: to this standard are available on the Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) website. Standard 1.2.5, the
L36@P3: guidance materials and other labelling standards should be consulted to understand the specific legal requirements
L37@P3: relating to date marking.
L38@P3: If food must be eaten within a certain period for health or safety reasons a use by date is required:
L39@P3: • The ‘health reason’ is applicable to food that is intended by the manufacturer to form the sole source of nutrition
L40@P3: for a person’s diet for a specified period. This will apply where storage affects the critical nutrient profile of
L41@P3: products such as infant formula or special dietary foods manufactured to provide the sole source of nutrition to
L42@P3: persons who are ill or are unable to eat normal foods.
L43@P3: • The ‘safety reason’ is applicable to food that can become microbiologically unsafe before the food noticeably
L44@P3: spoils. This will not apply to shelf stable, frozen or most raw foods but may apply to certain chilled ready-to-eat
L45@P3: foods, for example chilled meals and salads. Development of use by dates for safety reasons is discussed
L46@P3: below.
L47@P3: A best before date is applicable to food where deterioration affects consumer acceptance without impacting on
L48@P3: health and safety. Many product changes will affect consumer acceptance, including:
L49@P3: • rancidity
L50@P3: • texture changes
L51@P3: • moisture loss
L52@P3: • moisture gain
L53@P3: • staling
L54@P3: • flavour loss
L55@P3: • light induced changes
L56@P3: • enzymatic browning
L57@P3: • chemical browning, and
L58@P3: • microbial spoilage.
L59@P3: There is an exemption from best before date marking for products with a shelf life of two years or longer. A scan of
L60@P3: supermarket shelves identified few products that were not date marked. These included sugar, golden syrup,
L61@P3: treacle, honey, some soft drinks, canned fruit, canned vegetables, canned soup, vinegar, some canned fish and
L62@P3: some cook-in sauces. Apart from these products, a shelf life of two years or longer is uncommon. While is it not
L63@P3: mandatory in Australia some international food regulators recommend that all food be date marked to support ‘first
L64@P3: in – first out’ food consumption.

L106@P5: How is shelf life determined?
L107@P5: There are many answers to this question, depending on the type of deterioration that the product undergoes. The
L108@P5: NSW Food Authority has food safety as a primary objective and so considers reliable use by dates to be essential.
L109@P5: If use by or best before dates are overstated there is a substantial risk of damage to a business’s reputation and
L110@P5: brands. Food businesses must attend to all date marking requirements but this document will focus on the setting
L111@P5: of reliable use by dates.
L112@P5: Product development
L113@P5: Product development must be well advanced before a business can have confidence in shelf life estimates. The
L114@P5: business must be able to reliably produce homogeneous product with consistency from batch to batch. Physical
L115@P5: and chemical factors that impact on the capacity of bacteria to grow, such as pH, water activity and evenness of
L116@P5: mix (distribution of moisture, salt, preservative or food acid) must be well controlled. The physical, chemical and
L117@P5: microbiological profile of several batches of finished product must be established. The potential for product
L118@P5: contamination must be evaluated. The packaging materials must be identified.
L119@P5: Product stability and food safety
L120@P5: Initial studies are likely to consist of storage trials under the recommended storage conditions. Refrigerated storage
L121@P5: trials should be run at 5°C and under conditions of mild temperature abuse equal to what might be encountered in
L122@P5: the commercial cold chain. Products should be inspected at suitable times and samples tested for stability of the
L123@P5: critical physical and chemical characteristics. These trials also provide an opportunity to commence microbiological
L124@P5: tests for both spoilage organisms and the cold-tolerant pathogens named in the flow chart above. The trials should
L125@P5: continue beyond the targeted shelf life unless the product fails earlier.
L126@P5: Subtle changes during storage can be significant:
L127@P5: • Does pH change eg due to microbial growth or failing buffering systems?
L128@P5: • Is water redistributed within the product eg due to syneresis, condensation or a broken emulsion?
L129@P5: • Is the preservative system stable?
L130@P5: • Are there any signs of surface growth of microorganisms?
L131@P5: • Does any modified atmosphere persist for required times?
L132@P5: • Do chemicals migrate from the food-contact materials into the food?
L133@P5: • Are there any unforeseen changes to the product?
L134@P5: The trials should lead to an understanding of target levels and ranges for the critical physical and chemical
L135@P5: characteristics of the product over the intended shelf life. The worst case or least restrictive values (maximum or
L136@P5: minimum as the case may be) of the key chemical characteristics can then be compared to published values to
L137@P5: evaluate the opportunity for microbial growth, particularly for the key cold-tolerant pathogens.
L138@P5: The user guide to Standard 1.2.5 provides much of the information likely to be required to complete the evaluation.

L174@P7: Microbial growth models, such as ComBase, can also be used to illustrate the importance of using realistic rather
L175@P7: than ideal temperatures for storage trials. Figure 2 shows the hypothetical growth rate of Listeria monocytogenes in
L176@P7: food with a pH of 5.5 and salt content of 2%.
L177@P7: Figure 2: The importance of using realistic temperatures in storage trials
L178@P7: This figure demonstrates that Listeria monocytogenes, under conditions of mild thermal abuse— temperatures that
L179@P7: could well be encountered in the Australian cold chain—can grow (hypothetically) from a count of 10/g to about
L180@P7: 100,000/g within the shelf life of many chilled foods. The use of ideal temperatures in shelf life studies is bad
L181@P7: practice and likely to be misleading.
L182@P7: It must be noted that that model predictions are based on observations made in artificial growth media and
L183@P7: available product studies. To understand how a pathogen will behave in a specific product requires challenge tests.
L184@P7: Challenge tests
L185@P7: A challenge test is similar to the microbiological testing used in storage trials mentioned above. The difference is
L186@P7: that specific cold-tolerant pathogens are added to the product prior to packaging. Storage tests on products where
L187@P7: pathogenic bacteria are only occasionally present are likely to be misleading. The design and evaluation of
L188@P7: challenge tests should be done by an expert food microbiologist (NACMCF 2009).
L189@P7: If ‘growth – no growth’ is not clear from other studies then challenge tests can be very useful. Their use is not
L190@P7: required where it is obvious that either growth won’t occur or growth will occur. Challenge tests are used to clarify
L191@P7: areas of uncertainty and verify growth models for products with chemical characteristics near to the growth – no
L192@P7: growth border.
L193@P7: Cultures of pathogenic bacteria should never be introduced into a food processing factory. Challenge tests
L194@P7: should only be done in an off-site laboratory or research facility.

L209@P8: Expiry testing program
L210@P8: Retained samples from commercial batches of product should be tested for microbiological and chemical quality
L211@P8: upon expiry of shelf life. Newly launched products should be tested more frequently while proven products should
L212@P8: be tested occasionally. Samples for testing should be stored under realistic rather than ideal temperature
L213@P8: conditions. Expiry testing provides further assurance that manufacturing systems are under control.
L214@P8: Use by estimation – an auditable program
L215@P8: A series of linked documents can be used to provide objective evidence that use by dates are reliable.
L216@P8: Product specification: this should list the physical, chemical and microbiological characteristics of the product. In
L217@P8: some cases the document might specify both release (start of shelf life) and expiry (end of shelf life) criteria. The
L218@P8: specified criteria should be traceable by formulation / recipe / block code to product development records.
L219@P8: Even seemingly minor changes to ingredients, manufacturing procedures, packaging or distribution can have
L220@P8: adverse affects on product safety and use by dates. The shelf life of the revised product should be verified, for
L221@P8: example, by expiry testing of trial batches.
L222@P8: For the same reason, similar precautions should be taken when applying the same use by date to a family of
L223@P8: products with very similar characteristics. The product where growth is least restricted should be used in the shelf
L224@P8: life study.
L225@P8: The specification should also correlate with quality assurance test results for manufactured batches. The
L226@P8: specification should include recommended storage conditions and the shelf life. This should be traceable to the
L227@P8: shelf life study.
L228@P8: Shelf life study: this should summarise the storage trial results and any required microbial growth modelling and
L229@P8: challenge tests. The report should justify the shelf life estimate.
L230@P8: Expiry testing records: quality assurance test results on retention samples stored for the product’s shelf life at
L231@P8: realistic temperatures should verify the shelf life claims.
L232@P8: Determination of best before dates
L233@P8: There are potentially many questions to be answered about product deterioration and many approaches to getting
L234@P8: the answers. If products have a long shelf life then real time testing is not likely to fit in with product development
L235@P8: cycles. In many cases accelerated stability testing can be used to estimate shelf life; but technical expertise is a
L236@P8: necessity. Real time testing—after the product has been launched if necessary—should be used to verify estimated
L237@P8: best before dates.
L238@P8: Accelerated stability testing typically involves storing products at high temperature / humidity / light intensity or
L239@P8: similar. Products are tested following storage for different times and conditions. For some products, tests other than
L240@P8: those used for routine quality control might be required. The calculations to convert the results of testing into an
L241@P8: estimated shelf life are complex. Some businesses will require the services of a consulting chemist to supervise the
L242@P8-9: studies. 10
