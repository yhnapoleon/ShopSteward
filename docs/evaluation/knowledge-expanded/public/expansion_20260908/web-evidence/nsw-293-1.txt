L1@P0: Cooling potentially hazardous food
L2@P0: A common contributing factor to food poisoning in food businesses is incorrect temperature control.
L3@P0: This is when food is held for too long at temperatures where harmful food poisoning bacteria can
L4@P0: grow. The time food takes to cool (or reheat) is sometimes overlooked in food businesses and this is
L5@P0: when problems occur.
L6@P0: It is important that food businesses make sure cooked potentially hazardous food (PHF) has been
L7@P0: cooled in accordance with Standard 3.2.2, Clause 7(3) of the Food Standards Code (the Code).
L8@P0: Standard 3.2.2, Clause 7(3), Food Standards Code
L9@P0: A food business must, when cooling cooked potentially hazardous food, cool the food:
L10@P0: • within two hours – from 60°C to 21°C, and
L11@P0: • within a further four hours – from 21°C to 5°C,
L12@P0: unless the food business demonstrates that the cooling process used will not adversely affect the
L13@P0: microbiological safety of the food.
L14@P0: The correct cooling process
L15@P0: The cooling of cooked PHF needs to be as quick as possible to prevent the growth of pathogenic
L16@P0: bacteria to unsafe levels. The less time that cooked PHF remains at temperatures between 5°C and
L17@P0: 60°C during the cooling process, the less opportunity there will be for foodborne pathogens to grow.
L18@P0: It is possible that foodborne pathogens may be present in cooked food from spores that have survived
L19@P0: the cooking process. Cooking can activate the spores to become vegetative (live) cells, which then
L20@P0: grow.
L21@P0: If cooked PHF is left to cool too slowly, for example at room temperature or in large volumes in a cool
L22@P0: room, the vegetative cells can grow to dangerous levels. Re-heating the food may not reduce the
L23@P0: number of bacteria and may not destroy any bacterial toxins that may be produced.
L24@P0: Factors affecting cooling
L25@P0: The procedure used to cool PHF will depend on:
L26@P0: • • The size or amount of the food to be cooled – large amounts will cool slower than small amounts,
L27@P0: and cooling will be fastest on the food's surface and progressively slower towards its centre. Large
L28@P0: volumes of food may not sufficiently cool all the way through within the times and temperatures
L29@P0: specified, so it is important to portion food into smaller amounts.
L30@P0-1: • The density of the food (how solid/ liquid a food is) – the denser the food, the slower it will cool. Cooling potentially hazardous food

L32@P1: • The cooling capacity of the equipment – blast chillers will cool food much faster than refrigerators,
L33@P1: and over-stocking a refrigerator or placing large amounts of hot food into a refrigerator will reduce
L34@P1: its overall cooling capacity.
L35@P1: The cooling process
L36@P1: Note: The total cooling time of 6 hours only applies once the temperature of cooked potentially
L37@P1: hazardous food has dropped to 60°C after cooking.
L38@P1: Cooling times can be reduced by:
L39@P1: • cooking and cooling smaller amounts or portions placing food into large shallow containers to cool
L40@P1: (for example, 5 cm deep)
L41@P1: • using rapid-cooling equipment for example, a blast chiller)
L42@P1: • stirring liquid foods frequently (ensuring the stirring utensil has been cleaned and sanitised)
L43@P1: • using water or ice water baths
L44@P1: • allowing cool air to circulate around the container of food to be cooled – PHF should be cooled on
L45@P1: racks and not on the floor of a cool room
L46@P1: • adding ice as an ingredient.
L47@P1: Monitoring the cooling process
L48@P1: It is important that the temperature of food is monitored during cooling to ensure the procedure used is
L49@P1: effective. Food temperatures should be checked with a clean, sanitised thermometer in the part of the
L50@P1: food that will take the longest to cool, usually the centre. It is good practice to record both the
L51@P1: temperature and the time the temperature was taken to make sure the cooling process meets food
L52@P1: safety requirements.

L62@P2: Alternative cooling processes
L63@P2: Extended cooling times may be necessary when large cooked meats or other products need to be
L64@P2: cooled. Large volumes of food will not cool to below 5°C within the 6-hour requirement unless the
L65@P2: mass and volume of the food can be decreased. If this option is not available, food businesses will
L66@P2: need to demonstrate an alternative cooling process that will not adversely affect the microbiological
L67@P2: safety of the food.
L68@P2: Examples of cooling food safely
L69@P2: Example 1 – Rice
L70@P2: A restaurant's usual practice is to cook 12 cups of rice (the capacity of a rice cooker) daily and cool the
L71@P2: rice in a container in the cool room overnight for use the next day. However, when checking the
L72@P2: temperature at the centre of the cooked rice as it is cooling, it is discovered the rice is not actually
L73@P2: being cooled to 21°C in the first 2 hours and then to 5°C over the next 4 hours, as required.
L74@P2: The food business needs to work out a faster cooling method to ensure the rice is safe to eat. A
L75@P2: decision is made to divide the cooked rice in half and spread it on two separate, shallow trays for
L76@P2: cooling so that the rice can be correctly cooled all the way through within the 6-hour cooling
L77@P2: requirement. The business documents this process in its operating procedure and consistently uses
L78@P2: this new cooling method for rice.
L79@P2: Example 2 – Gravy
L80@P2: A food business prepares a large batch of gravy in the morning to use later that day. The pan of gravy
L81@P2: is placed in an ice slurry and then stirred regularly. The temperature is checked several times over the
L82@P2: next few hours to ensure cooling is within the required timeframe.
