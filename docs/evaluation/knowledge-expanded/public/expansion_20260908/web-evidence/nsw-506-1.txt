L22@P2: Foreword
L23@P2: Sous vide translates from the French as ‘under vacuum’. In culinary terms it has come to mean a process of cooking
L24@P2: under vacuum in sealed pouches (oxygen barrier bags), at precise (and sometimes low) temperatures and often for
L25@P2: long times. Sous vide can be used to prepare foods with an extended shelf life for retail sale or use in food service.
L26@P2: However, sous vide can also be used to prepare foods with qualities that cannot be achieved when using traditional
L27@P2: cooking techniques. Some sous vide recipes result in food that remains raw or undercooked.
L28@P2: This document includes information on the service of raw and undercooked foods. This information is provided in
L29@P2: response to a growing trend for sous vide use by restaurants and to encourage the use of safer choices for this type of
L30@P2: product: it is not an endorsement of the service of raw or undercooked foods. The onus of delivering a safe product to
L31@P2: the consumer resides with the business.
L32@P2: Standard 3.2.2 of the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code (Food Standards Code) states that when
L33@P2: processing food, a food business must:
L34@P2: where a process step is needed to reduce to safe levels any pathogens that may be present in the food, use a
L35@P2: process step that is reasonably known to achieve the microbiological safety of the food.
L36@P2: Cooking is a process known to achieve the microbiological safety of food and by choosing to serve raw or
L37@P2: undercooked animal foods, restaurateurs must acknowledge that there will be a residual public health and business
L38@P2: risk.
L39@P2: Section 1.6 of this document provides details on the public health risks of raw and undercooked foods. As an
L40@P2: alternative to the service of raw or undercooked foods, chefs should first consider the service of cooked food prepared
L41@P2-3: using times and temperatures outlined in Table 2 or Table 3.4
L42@P3: Executive summary
L43@P3: There are several types of sous vide foods. This document is about food safety hazards of cook serve and short term
L44@P3: cook chill sous vide foods. Some issues are common to the extended shelf life sous vide foods but guidance on the
L45@P3: preparation of these foods is readily available.
L46@P3: Sous vide seems like a new food service technology but it has a history spanning nearly five decades in France and
L47@P3: nearly three decades in other international marketplaces. It finds application in many market segments including
L48@P3: extended shelf life ready meals. It is also found in catering companies, restaurants and, increasingly, sous vide has
L49@P3: been embraced by home cooks.
L50@P3: While there is little evidence of sous vide food causing outbreaks of foodborne illness1, there is a reason why sous
L51@P3: vide causes concern to regulators. There are foreseeable hazards in three broad areas that must be managed:
L52@P3: 1. Foods held in the temperature danger zone, defined as between 5°C and 60°C by the Food Standards Code, for
L53@P3: extended periods are potentially subject to bacterial growth. For example, during low temperature long time (LT
L54@P3: LT) cooking processes, product cooling or product reheating.
L55@P3: 2. Foods stored under refrigeration for extended periods are potentially subject to growth by cold tolerant pathogens.
L56@P3: The growth of Listeria monocytogenes is a risk as is growth and toxin production by non-proteolytic Clostridium
L57@P3: botulinum.
L58@P3: 3. Food cooked at low temperatures for short periods will, in essence, remain raw and pathogenic bacteria, parasites
L59@P3: and viruses, if present, might survive.
L60@P3: If you choose to include on your menu foods that remain essentially raw, you must make clear the health implications
L61@P3: the food could pose to the consumer. New practitioners of sous vide must be aware of the food safety risks and avoid
L62@P3: overly experimental applications of the technology. Today’s leaders in sous vide produce quality food without losing
L63@P3: sight of food safety.
L64@P3: 1 There were 3 foodborne illness cases reported in 2014
L65@P3: The risks associated with sous vide foods can be reduced if:
L66@P3: • thinner portions of food are prepared so that heating and cooling are rapid.
L67@P3: • overcrowding of product in the water bath is avoided. Overcrowding can affect cooking times and increase
L68@P3: the chance of cold spots occurring. The time of cooking will need to be increased to compensate.
L69@P3: • water bath temperatures of at least 55°C are used so that the growth of Clostridium perfringens is first
L70@P3: prevented and then destruction of the cells commences.
L71@P3: • the time food is held below 54.5°C during cooking is limited to six hours.
L72@P3: • commercial equipment with adequate heating capacity and excellent temperature control is used.
L73@P3: • water and/or food temperatures are checked using a tip sensitive digital thermometer that is accurate to
L74@P3: 0.1°C.
L75@P3: • prepared foods are not stored for extended times unless processes have been validated.
L76@P3: • risks are not compounded. Cooking large portions of mechanically tenderised meat for extended times at low
L77@P3-4: temperatures would be irresponsible.5
L78@P4: Definition
L79@P4: 2-hour/4-hour rule The rule provides options for what can be done safely with ready-to-eat potentially
L80@P4: hazardous food, depending on how long it has been at temperatures between 5°C and
L81@P4: 60°C.
L82@P4: The time between 5°C and 60°C is cumulative — that means you need to add up every
L83@P4: time the food has been out of the fridge, including during preparation, storage, transport
L84@P4: and display.
L85@P4: If the total time is:
L86@P4: Less than 2 hours, the food may be used immediately or refrigerated for later use,
L87@P4: Between 2 and 4 hours, the food must be used immediately (can’t be put back in the
L88@P4: fridge), and
L89@P4: 4 hours or longer, the food must be thrown out.
L90@P4: It provides an alternative to temperature control specified in Standard 3.2.2 of the Food
L91@P4: Standards Code (i.e. food must be kept at or below 5°C, or at or above 60°C).

L208@P8: Sous vide processing
L209@P8: Sous vide translates from the French as ‘under vacuum’. In culinary terms it has come to mean a process of cooking
L210@P8: under vacuum in sealed pouches (oxygen barrier bags), at precise (and sometimes low) temperatures and often for
L211@P8: long times. Creed (1998) quotes the widely accepted definition developed by the Sous Vide Advisory Committee
L212@P8: (SVAC):
L213@P8: Sous vide (also known as Cuisine en Papillote Sous Vide) is an interrupted3 catering system in which raw or parcooked food is sealed in a vacuumed laminated plastic pouch or container, heat treated by controlled cooking,
L214@P8: rapidly cooled, and then reheated for service after a period of chilled storage.
L215@P8: Sous vide can be used to prepare foods with an extended shelf life for retail sale or use in food service. In these
L216@P8: examples the ‘interrupted catering system’ is the relevant characteristic. However, sous vide can also be used to
L217@P8: prepare foods with qualities that cannot be achieved when using traditional cooking techniques. In this instance ‘inpouch’ cooking under precise and often sensitive conditions provides the sought-after characteristics and refrigerated
L218@P8: or frozen storage might not be part of the process.
L219@P8: The wide range of products known as sous vide can be divided into four broad categories.
L220@P8: • Processed 90/10. Heated to reduce the numbers of non-proteolytic Clostridium botulinum by 99.9999%. This
L221@P8: more robust process is likely to be used for retail ready meals or extended shelf life cook chill foods. One
L222@P8: conventional process calls for the slowest heating part of the food to be exposed to a combination of
L223@P8: temperature and time equivalent to 90°C for 10 minutes. Lower temperatures can be used but exposure times
L224@P8: increase significantly.
L225@P8: • Processed 70/2. Heated to reduce the numbers of Listeria monocytogenes by 99.9999%. This is commonly
L226@P8: known as food pasteurisation and it could be used by restaurants, caterers and some manufacturers of
L227@P8: extended shelf life foods. One conventional process calls for the slowest heating part of the food to be exposed
L228@P8: to a combination of temperature and time equivalent to 70°C for 2 minutes. Lower temperatures can be used but
L229@P8: exposure times increase significantly.
L230@P8: • Cooked. This term mainly refers to taste, texture and appearance but it has also become associated with
L231@P8: certain minimum heating processes for meats. Cooked but not necessarily pasteurised foods will be
L232@P8: encountered in restaurants and home sous vide. One process specified by the US Food Safety Inspection
L233@P8: Service (FSIS, 2011) calls for the slowest heating part of the food to be heated to 63°C followed by a 3- minute
L234@P8: rest time. Lower temperatures can be used to prepare rare meats but exposure times increase significantly.
L235@P8: Higher temperatures are specified for poultry and mechanically tenderised, injected, comminuted or stuffed
L236@P8: meats. Extended times can be used to tenderise cheaper cuts of meat.
L237@P8: • Lightly processed. Some sous vide recipes do not result in cooked food. Any contaminant and pathogenic
L238@P8: bacteria, parasites or viruses that might be present are likely to survive.
L239@P8: In food service operations sous vide foods will generally be cooked in a finely controlled circulating water bath or a
L240@P8: combination steam/forced convection oven. Larger catering companies or manufacturers preparing ready meals might
L241@P8: use an enclosed vessel (sometimes called a retort) with a water cascade system to first heat and then cool the
L242@P8: pouches of food.

L244@P8-9: Interrupted – implies a holding step, as in cook chill as opposed to cook serve for immediate use.10
L245@P9: Concerns about food safety
L246@P9: Sous vide has been embraced by a number of celebrity chefs and the use of the technique is spreading in culinary
L247@P9: circles. A similar surge in popularity of sous vide in New York CBD restaurants, in part, resulted in an effective ban on
L248@P9: ‘reduced oxygen packaging’ (ROP) pending individual approval from the city’s Department of Health and Mental
L249@P9: Hygiene (NY Times, 2006). One concern was that chefs trained in sous vide, aware of the hazards, had been joined
L250@P9: by those who were not aware. The Department was concerned that ROP suppressed the growth of spoilage bacteria
L251@P9: while providing a suitable environment for pathogens such as L. monocytogenes and C. botulinum. To gain approval to
L252@P9: use ROP, food establishments were required to have a food safety plan for each food item and multiple barriers
L253@P9: against the growth of Listeria and Clostridium were required (e.g. pH, water activity).
L254@P9: Retail sous vide foods such as ready meals and, more generally, vacuum packed and modified atmosphere chilled
L255@P9: foods, have a good record of food safety. The Institute of Food Research (Peck et al, 2006) found no cases of
L256@P9: foodborne botulism could be attributed to correctly stored commercial chilled foods in the UK or overseas. Guidance
L257@P9: on the preparation of sous vide ready meals and cook chill foods generally is readily available—see Cox and Bauler
L258@P9: (2008), UKFSA (2008) and FSAI (2006). These documents do not address food safety risks for ready meals and other
L259@P9: extended shelf life foods, although many of the issues of restaurant sous vide overlap with those for extended shelf life
L260@P9: foods.
L261@P9: The extent of foodborne illness attributable to restaurant prepared sous vide is not as clear, but reports of foodborne
L262@P9: illness are at least rare, although some reports of illness explicitly naming sous vide foods have been found in the
L263@P9: scientific literature. In 2014, Salmonella Enteritidis and sous vide were implicated in complaints against two
L264@P9: restaurants in Canada. Two of the three cases were from one restaurant and sous vide eggs were determined to be
L265@P9: the cause, while sous vide duck breast was determined to be the likely cause for the other case from the second
L266@P9: restaurant. Investigations at both restaurants revealed inadequate sous vide cooking practices that did not fully cook
L267@P9: the food (McIntyre et al, 2017).
L268@P9: There are many hazards that must be managed with sous vide food. Sous vide literature has focussed on botulism,
L269@P9: but there are other potential hazards that have received less attention. Sous vide hazards fall into three areas:
L270@P9: • Foods held in the temperature danger zone (between 5°C and 60°C) for extended periods are potentially
L271@P9: subject to bacterial growth, for example, during
L272@P9: - low temperature long time (LT LT) cooking processes,
L273@P9: - product cooling, or
L274@P9: - product reheating.
L275@P9: • Foods stored under refrigeration for extended periods are potentially subject to growth by cold tolerant
L276@P9: pathogens. The growth of L. monocytogenes is a risk, as is growth and toxin production by non-proteolytic
L277@P9: C. botulinum.
L278@P9: • Food cooked at low temperatures for short periods will, in essence, remain raw and pathogenic bacteria, viruses
L279@P9: and parasites, if present, might survive.
L280@P9: The established knowledge relating to food safety should not be ignored by experimental chefs. This knowledge has
L281@P9: mainly been acquired—the hard way—through food poisoning incidents. Today’s leaders in sous vide produce new
L282@P9-10: and interesting food without losing sight of food safety.11
L283@P10: A food safety issue that may be overlooked in sous vide is the proper cleaning of all sous vide equipment (circulators,
L284@P10: immersions baths, etc). The frequency and step by step instructions for cleaning should be part of the food safety
L285@P10: manual and all staff should be trained.
L286@P10: New technologies such as sous vide apps on smart phones are now widely available. These should be used with
L287@P10: caution and the user should still fully understand the food safety implications of using the sous vide method. A few
L288@P10: things to remember when using an app for sous vide:
L289@P10: • make sure that you have the latest version (apps are updated frequently and cooking time and temperatures
L290@P10: may also be updated),
L291@P10: • ensure you are using an app that is compatible with the type of immersion circulator you are using (some
L292@P10: brands have apps for their specific products), and
L293@P10: • ensure the recipe you are following is a sous vide recipe, as some apps offer other types of recipe instructions.
L294@P10: Food safety hazards
L295@P10: 1.1 Low temperature cooking – bacterial growth
L296@P10: Scientific literature on bacterial growth in low temperature cooking processes is quite limited. The Food Safety and
L297@P10: Inspection Service of the United States Department of Agriculture (FSIS, 1999) mentions the risk of ‘slow come up
L298@P10: time’ for certain meat and poultry products. The guideline states that ‘dwell times of greater than 6 hours in the 50°F
L299@P10: (10°C) to 130°F (54.4°C) range should be viewed as especially hazardous, as this temperature range can foster the
L300@P10: growth of many pathogens of concern’. Food can dwell in this temperature range due to ‘slow come up time’, or by
L301@P10: being held for an inordinate amount of time in that range. The guideline notes that the multiplication of pathogens can
L302@P10: be so great then even recooking may be ineffective in making the product safe. Toxigenic bacteria can release toxins
L303@P10: into the products. Some of the toxins, such as those of Staphylococcus aureus, are extremely heat stable and are not
L304@P10: inactivated by normal recooking temperatures.
L305@P10: The Food Code (US Food and Drug Administration, 2009) includes several clauses on time as a public health control.
L306@P10: Clause 3–501.19 (1) (B), is similar in intent to the ‘2-hour/4-hour rule’ for temperature control outlined in Food
L307@P10: Standards Australia New Zealand website (FSANZ, n.d). The US Food Code allows for a maximum of four hours
L308@P10: between 5°C and 57°C, whereas the comparable Australian guideline temperatures are 5°C and 60°C.
L309@P10: Low temperature long time (LT LT) cooking is not new. Braising and crockpot are familiar examples used by some
L310@P10: steak houses to prepare prime roasts. The main benefit is tenderisation of cheaper meat cuts. Care must be taken if
L311@P10: unusually low temperatures are used to achieve an undercooked or rare product.
L312@P10: The main food safety risks of LT LT cooking are Clostridium perfringens and bacteria that form potentially hazardous
L313@P10: heat stable toxins in food such as Bacillus cereus and S. aureus. C. perfringens appears to be the pathogen best
L314@P10: adapted to growth during processing of sous vide meats (Willardsen et al, 1978) and if this organism is effectively
L315@P10: controlled, the risks from the other bacteria will be minimal.
L316@P10: The emergence of LT LT cooking can result in some foods being held at temperatures for periods of time that do not
L317@P10: comply with Australian or US guidelines. For example, mathematician and published author on sous vide, Douglas
L318@P10: Baldwin (2011, 2012, 2022), includes some recipes (tables of cooking times) where the combination of temperature
L319@P10-11: and time is outside the guidelines.12
L320@P11: Baldwin only tabulates times that comply with the US Food Code four hour limit for foods that are served
L321@P11: unpasteurised. However, for foods that are pasteurised during cooking, he allows up to six hours to reach 54.5°C. This
L322@P11: combination of time and temperature challenges two familiar concepts—the ‘temperature danger zone’ and the ‘2-
L323@P11: hour/4-hour rule’.
L324@P11: However, based on the comments of The Food Safety and Inspection Service of the United States Department of
L325@P11: Agriculture (FSIS, 1999) and the studies by Willardsen et al (1978), the NSW Food Authority accepts that any risk to
L326@P11: food safety is low.
L327@P11: Hands-on: Sous vide low temperature long time (LT LT) cooking
L328@P11: This section is about LT LT production of cooked foods. Table 1 and Table 2 have been prepared by Baldwin
L329@P11: (personal communications, 2011, 2012). The times shown allow for product heating followed by pasteurisation (Table
L330@P11: 1) or cooking (Table 2). The times are estimates and they are prepared using information about heat transfer through
L331@P11: foods and the lethal impacts of cooking temperatures on bacteria that are significant causes of foodborne illness.
L332@P11: Where water baths are used it is important that they return to the set temperature quickly after the addition of food.
L333@P11: Temperature recovery will be faster if a commercial water bath heater/stirrer with a high power heating element is
L334@P11: used. Precise temperature control is important and high technology controllers (often labelled or described as PID
L335@P11: controllers) are specified. Operating temperatures should be checked with an accurate, calibrated thermometer.
L336@P11: Table 3 records the holding time required to pasteurise the food after the target temperature has been achieved. It can
L337@P11: be used when pouches have in-pack thermometers, for example, in combination steamer/forced convection ovens,
L338@P11: high temperature water baths or water cascade systems.
L339@P11: The estimates calculated by Baldwin can be used for cook serve food or food stored for a few days. Extended shelf life
L340@P11: cook chill foods or foods with unusual recipes require expert and tailored thermal process development. Estimates of
L341@P11: lethality vary depending on the characteristics of food used in the trial. Baldwin’s guidance on his website (2022)
L342@P11: provides a good example. Baldwin presents calculations for lean fish based on observations made on cod, and for
L343@P11: fatty fish based on observations made on salmon. Process times for salmon are longer than for cod. It is important to
L344@P11: note that food composition affects pasteurisation time.
L345@P11: Many factors can affect lethality of pathogens, for example:
L346@P11: • Cox and Bauler (2008) note that the presence of lysozyme and other related enzymes might influence the
L347@P11: lethality rate.
