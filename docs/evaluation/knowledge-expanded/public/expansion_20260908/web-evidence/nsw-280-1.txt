L3@P0: Why do I need a sanitiser?
L4@P0: Bacteria that cause illness (Salmonella for example) are commonly found on raw foods such as raw
L5@P0: meat. The bacteria on these foods can be transferred to surfaces and equipment that comes into
L6@P0: contact with them. Effective cleaning and sanitising in your food business helps protect you and your
L7@P0: customers against the spread of bacteria and can stop your customers from getting sick.
L8@P0: Chemical sanitisers are applied to food contact equipment after cleaning to kill bacteria that survive
L9@P0: the cleaning process. A surface needs to be thoroughly cleaned before it is sanitised, see Cleaning
L10@P0: and sanitising in retail food businesses for more information.
L11@P0: It is a requirement that any equipment that comes into contact with food, such as kitchen benches,
L12@P0: knives, chopping boards, eating and drinking utensils, pots, and meat slicers must be in a clean and
L13@P0: sanitary condition¹.
L14@P0: To be effective in a food premises, a sanitiser should kill 99.99% to 99.999% of bacteria. There are
L15@P0: many different types of sanitisers. The most common chemical sanitisers are chlorine-based products,
L16@P0: such as hypochlorite (also known as bleach), and quaternary ammonium products. Other chemicals
L17@P0: may be effective if correctly prepared and used. If bleach is used as a chemical sanitiser only plain
L18@P0: unscented bleach should be used.

L22@P1: Where can I get a chemical sanitiser?
L23@P1: Chemical sanitisers are available from many different commercial suppliers, for example chemical,
L24@P1: catering and cleaning suppliers, and are usually quaternary ammonium products. Bleach is widely
L25@P1: available in retail supermarkets.
L26@P1: How do I use a sanitiser?
L27@P1: Different brands of sanitiser vary greatly in their instructions for use. The manufacturer’s instructions
L28@P1: should always be followed. Clear instructions must be provided either on the product label (see
L29@P1: example in Figure 3) or a product information sheet. If instructions are not clearly provided with the
L30@P1: product, advice should be sought from the supplier or manufacturer and an alternate product sourced
L31@P1: if necessary.
L32@P1: Figure 3. Example of a sanitiser label.
L33@P1: The user must always READ THE LABEL (and/or the product information sheet and Safety Data
L34@P1: Sheet) and understand the following about the sanitiser:
L35@P1: • Is it safe to use on food contact equipment?
L36@P1: • Is it a ready-to-use product and doesn’t require diluting?
L37@P1: • What are the dilution rates if it needs to be diluted?
L38@P1: • What is the ‘contact time’ (i.e., the amount of time a sanitiser must be in contact with an item for it
L39@P1: to work)?
L40@P1: • Is rinsing required?
L41@P1-2: • What is the shelf life of the concentrated and diluted products? Chemical sanitisers – advice for food businesses

L43@P2: Some sanitisers can be used as received and are suitable for spray and wipe activities without
L44@P2: dilution. These ready-to-use sanitisers are generally unsuitable for equipment that requires immersion
L45@P2: (dipping) in a sink as they cannot be diluted.
L46@P2: Food businesses that use bleach as a sanitiser must follow the dilution instructions in Table 1 below.
L47@P2: The contact time for bleach varies depending upon the product, concentration, water temperature and
L48@P2: other factors (such as pH). The manufacturer’s instructions should always be followed in this regard.
L49@P2: As a guide, to be effective, bleach requires up to 30 seconds before the food contact equipment is
L50@P2: ready for use. Bleach is not required to be rinsed off, as long as the dilution instructions have been
L51@P2: followed.
L52@P2: Applying a chemical sanitiser to a food contact surface
L53@P2: • Only use products with clear and informative labels or product information sheets so the
L54@P2: instructions can be easily checked
L55@P2: • All surfaces to be sanitised must be clean and rinsed first as sanitisers do not work well in the
L56@P2: presence of food residues or other detergents
L57@P2: • Sanitisers should be made to the correct dilution rate (too low or too high is ineffective) and used
L58@P2: with the correct water temperature and for the correct contact time, as specified in the
L59@P2: manufacturer’s instructions
L60@P2: • Because the active chemical loses strength over time, a fresh batch of sanitiser should be made
L61@P2: every 24 hours, or as specified in the manufacturer's instructions
L62@P2: • Ensure that sanitising spray bottles are labelled correctly for example the name of the product,
L63@P2: when it was made up, et cet era
L64@P2: • All surfaces to be sanitised should be completely covered with the sanitising solution using
L65@P2: immersion (dipping) in a sink or using a spray
L66@P2: • Special attention should be given to equipment with surfaces that are difficult to get to, such as
L67@P2: stab mixers, blenders, meat slicers and can openers
L68@P2: • Equipment may need to be dismantled, per manufacturer’s instructions, to access the parts
L69@P2: that need to be sanitised
L70@P2: • Equipment that cannot be effectively cleaned and sanitised should not be used in a food
L71@P2: business
L72@P2: • After sanitising, utensils and food surfaces should be thoroughly dried:
L73@P2: • Wet surfaces pick up dirt or other contaminants more easily than dry surfaces.
L74@P2: • Air drying is preferable otherwise use clean, dry, single-use towels
L75@P2: • Care should be taken not to re-contaminate sanitised utensils and equipment, e.g. by
L76@P2-3: ensuring they are stored using clean hands and in a clean and sanitary place.Chemical sanitisers – advice for food businesses
