L94@P4: Introduction
L95@P4: Standard 3.2.2 of the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code (Food Standards Code) defines
L96@P4: potentially hazardous food (PHF) as “food that has to be kept at certain temperatures to minimise the
L97@P4: growth of any pathogenic microorganisms that may be present in the food or to prevent the formation of
L98@P4: toxins in the food”. Potentially hazardous foods are also referred to as ‘Time/temperature control for
L99@P4: safety (TCS) foods’ in other reference documents. The phrase does not mean a naturally dangerous
L100@P4: food such as a poisonous mushroom.
L101@P4: The need for time/temperature control is primarily determined by the potential for contamination with and
L102@P4: survival of pathogenic microorganisms of concern and the potential for subsequent growth and/or toxin
L103@P4: production. Factors affecting microbial growth include moisture content (water activity, aw), acidity (pH),
L104@P4: nutrient content, antimicrobials, atmosphere of packaging, processing steps and storage conditions. If
L105@P4: the combination of these factors creates a favourable environment and the food is not kept under
L106@P4: temperature control, microorganisms can grow, and some may form toxins. If the levels of pathogenic
L107@P4: microorganisms or toxins reach unsafe levels, foodborne illness may occur.
L108@P4: Clause 8 of Standard 3.2.2 requires that a food business, when displaying potentially hazardous food,
L109@P4: display it under temperature control. This clause is simple and logical. However, when it comes to
L110@P4: inspecting food on display the situation is not always as simple. In some cases, it is not possible to
L111@P4: determine whether a food is potentially hazardous during an inspection. While the food business has the
L112@P4: responsibility to demonstrate that it is handling food safely, many small businesses are not equipped to
L113@P4: do so.
L114@P4: This document includes information on factors that can be used to assess the hazard, lists of food types
L115@P4: where the nature of the hazard can be determined and provides information on what laboratory work can
L116@P4-5: be done to provide clarity when the nature of the hazard is not clear.Potentially hazardous foods – Foods that require temperature control for safety

L147@P5: would make the end product potentially hazardous.

L150@P6: What food is not potentially hazardous?
L151@P6: If a food does not support the growth of a pathogen or toxin production, then it is not potentially
L152@P6: hazardous.
L153@P6: Some foods in their natural state do not support pathogen growth because their intrinsic properties
L154@P6: create an unfavourable environment, for example, foods that are naturally acidic. Other foods may have
L155@P6: been processed in a way that minimises microbial growth (for example, dried, salted, acidified) or has
L156@P6: eliminated microorganisms in the food (such as commercially sterilised food). In addition, the use of
L157@P6: certain chemical additives such as nitrites, sulphites or specialised packaging may minimise microbial
L158@P6: growth in food.
L159@P6: Examples of food types considered to be not potentially hazardous1
L160@P6: Biscuits and crackers Fruit cake Plain cakes
L161@P6: Bottled marinades (before
L162@P6: opening)
L163@P6: Fruit juices Raw whole fruit and vegetables
L164@P6: Bottled pasta sauces (before
L165@P6: opening)
L166@P6: Hard cheeses Salad dressings
L167@P6: Bottled salsas (before opening) Honey and jam Sauces – Asian/soy, ketchup
L168@P6: style
L169@P6: Confectionery Nuts in the shell Salted dried meats
L170@P6: Dried fruit Peanut butter Unopened canned foods
L171@P6: Dry goods Pickles Yoghurts
L172@P6: Fermented dried meats Plain breads and bread rolls
L173@P6: Note:
L174@P6: Foods in the above list do not support the growth of pathogenic bacteria or the formation of toxins in the
L175@P6: food. However:
L176@P6: 1. Many of the products require refrigerated storage to prevent food spoilage or maintain quality to
L177@P6: achieve the stated shelf-life.
L178@P6: 2. Some foods might contain certain pathogenic microorganisms but not support their growth.
L179@P6: Although by definition such foods are not “potentially hazardous”, they can still be a food safety
L180@P6: hazard. For example, food contaminated with viruses such as hepatitis A or norovirus can cause
L181@P6: foodborne illness even though the pathogen does not multiply in the food.
L182@P6: 3. The above foods might become potentially hazardous when the food is opened or altered in
L183@P6: some ways. For example, opening a can of food, cutting up a whole melon, slicing cured meat,
L184@P6: cooking vegetables or reconstituting dry foods.

L187@P7: Regulations and Guidelines
L188@P7: The Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code
L189@P7: Standard 3.2.2 – Food Safety Practices and General Requirements defines potentially hazardous food
L190@P7: (PHF) as “food that has to be kept at certain temperatures to minimise the growth of any pathogenic
L191@P7: microorganisms that may be present in the food or to prevent the formation of toxins in the food”.
L192@P7: The associated definition of temperature control is important. Standard 3.2.2 defines temperature control
L193@P7: as maintaining food as a temperature of –
L194@P7: a. 5°C, or below if this is necessary to minimise the growth of infectious or toxigenic microorganisms
L195@P7: in the food so that the microbiological safety of the food will not be adversely affected for the time
L196@P7: the food is at that temperature; or
L197@P7: b. 60°C or above; or
L198@P7: c. Another temperature – if the food business demonstrates that maintenance of the food at this
L199@P7: temperature for the period of time for which it will be so maintained, will not adversely affect the
L200@P7: microbiological safety of the food.
L201@P7: The 2-hour/4-hour rule
L202@P7: The 2-hour/4-hour rule is an alternative method that has been designed to help businesses deal with
L203@P7: some of the practicalities of handling refrigerated potentially hazardous ready-to-eat food. Studies have
L204@P7: been done to show that food can be held out of temperature control for short periods of time without
L205@P7: significantly increasing the risk of food poisoning.
L206@P7: The principal concepts of the 2-hour/4-hour rule are that when potentially hazardous ready-to-eat food
L207@P7: has been kept between 5°C and 60°C for:
L208@P7: • up to 2 hours, it can be refrigerated below 5°C, or kept hot above 60°C, or used immediately.
L209@P7: • between 2 and 4 hours, it must be used immediately.
L210@P7: • up to a total of 4 hours or more, it must be thrown out.
L211@P7: It is important to note that the time is cumulative; all time periods where the food is at temperatures
L212@P7: between 5°C and 60°C need to be counted.
L213@P7: If a food business wishes to maintain potentially hazardous food between the temperatures of 5°C and
L214@P7: 60°C for time periods longer than the two hours and four hours specified above, the food business must
L215@P7: demonstrate that the extension of time will not compromise the safety of the food.
L216@P7: For more information on the use of the 2-hour/4-hour rule, please refer to the ‘Guidance on the 2-hour/4-
L217@P7: hour rule’ document on the Food Authority’s website foodauthority.nsw.gov.au/resource-centre.

L230@P8: How to determine if foods are potentially hazardous
L231@P8: Water activity (aw) as the control factor for pathogen growth
L232@P8: The water activity of a food describes the degree to which water is “bound” in the food, its availability to
L233@P8: participate in chemical/biochemical reactions and its ability to make possible the growth of
L234@P8: microorganisms (IFT/FDA, 2001). In foods, it ranges from 0.10 (dry products, like crackers) to 1.00 (very
L235@P8: moist). Most fresh foods have aw values that are close to the optimum growth level of most
L236@P8: microorganisms (0.97 to 0.99) (Appendix 1).
L237@P8: Appendix 2 shows the minimum aw at which foodborne pathogenic bacteria can grow or produce toxin.
L238@P8: The benchmark organism is Staphylococcus aureus which can grow at aw 0.83 and produce toxin at
L239@P8: 0.88.
L240@P8: pH as the control factor for pathogen growth
L241@P8: The pH of a food relates to its acidity. Most foods such as meat, fish and vegetables are slightly acidic,
L242@P8: while most fruits are moderately acidic. It is well known that groups of microorganisms have a pH
L243@P8: optimum, minimum and maximum for growth in foods.
L244@P8: Appendix 3 shows the minimum pH at which foodborne pathogenic bacteria can grow or produce toxin.
L245@P8: The benchmark vegetative bacteria are S. aureus which can grow at pH 4.0 and produce toxin at 4.6 and
L246@P8: Yersinia enterocolitica and Salmonella spp. both of which can grow at pH 4.2. The spore-forming
L247@P8: bacteria of interest is Clostridium botulinum which can grow and produce toxin down to pH 4.6.
L248@P8: Interactions between aw and pH
L249@P8: The tables below (IFT/FDA, 2001) provide a useful advice on some combinations of pH and water
L250@P8: activity which, taken together, will prevent microbial growth or spore outgrowth. Those combinations that
L251@P8: lead to “non-TCS” are also not PHF. If the combination is denoted by a “?”, it indicates that the product is
L252@P8: potentially unsafe without refrigeration. Note that some individual foods have characteristics that prevent
L253@P8: pathogen growth at conditions of pH and water activity that are less severe than those indicated in the
L254@P8: tables.
L255@P8: Care should be taken when analysing multicomponent foods because measurements of aw, pH, or
L256@P8: antimicrobials of the whole food may not reflect the actual value of the components or at the interface
L257@P8: among the different components. In these cases, the parameters should be measured at the interface

L293@P11: Individual product assessment
L294@P11: The IFT/FDA report includes details of individual product assessment. The steps set out in the report
L295@P11: should be undertaken under the guidance of a trained food technologist or food scientist. Laboratory
L296@P11: analysis is required to complete the assessment.
L297@P11: Step 1
L298@P11: Conduct a pH and water activity (aw) testing. If the product is not homogeneous, measurements of pH
L299@P11: and aw are required in triplicate for each component. The assessment of a food that is not homogenous
L300@P11: will be based on the “weakest link” (i.e. the highest risk).
L301@P11: Step 2
L302@P11: Evaluate the results of testing according to the appropriate IFT/FDA table (see page 10).
L303@P11: • Use Table 1 if the product is processed (e.g. cooked) to control vegetative cells and protected
L304@P11: from recontamination.
L305@P11: • Use Table 2 if the product is not treated to control vegetative cells or treated but not protected
L306@P11: from recontamination.
L307@P11: If the product is classified as non-TCS the assessment is complete.
L308@P11: Step 3
L309@P11: If the product is in the query area, then time or temperature-controlled display is required. Further studies
L310@P11: can be undertaken, if necessary, but time or temperature controls are often better options. The studies
L311@P11: are complex and expensive and require input from an experienced food technologist/scientist. Product
L312@P11: assessment should consider factors such as:
L313@P11: • Potential pathogens
L314@P11: • Intrinsic factors such as preservatives, acidulants, nutrients, humectants
L315@P11: • Extrinsic factors such as packaging, modified atmosphere, shelf life
L316@P11: • Effectiveness of the processing control of pathogens
L317@P11: • Possible post-process recontamination
L318@P11: • Historical information on the safety of similar or related products
L319@P11: • The consultant could also use microbial growth models to evaluate the likely success of
L320@P11: challenge studies.
L321@P11: Step 4
L322@P11: Microbial challenge studies tests should be undertaken if there is a reasonable likelihood of success.
L323@P11: ISO 20976-1:2019 specifies protocols for conducting microbiological challenge tests for growth studies
L324@P11: on vegetative and spore-forming bacteria in raw materials and intermediate or end products. Products
L325@P11: passing a properly designed challenge test are considered to have demonstrated alternative compliance
L326@P11: as long as controlled display times and temperatures do not exceed those employed in a successful
L327@P11: challenge trial.
L328@P11: Note 1: The results of microbiological testing of the food product are useful for verification of the
L329@P11: assessment but they do not stand alone. Good results might provide a level of comfort about product
L330@P11: safety, but they do not necessarily address all the inherent risks.
L331@P11: Note 2: The history of the product alone does not confirm product safety. The history must be
L332@P11-12: underpinned by a sound scientific evaluation.Potentially hazardous foods – Foods that require temperature control for safety

L334@P12: Verifying that foods are not potentially hazardous
L335@P12: Food businesses are responsible to determine which foods they serve, or sell are potentially hazardous.
L336@P12: Authorised officers require evidence of the determination, for example (but not limited to) a validation
L337@P12: study, analytical testing results or the result of a challenge testing.
L338@P12: Food businesses are also required to substantiate food safety controls when handling PHF.
L339@P12: Common issues
L340@P12: Warm food (less than 60°C) in a bain marie
L341@P12: A correctly adjusted bain marie can hold food at 60°to 65°C without generating too much steam. If food
L342@P12: on display is less than 60°C, the unit could be set too low, or the operator might have used the bain
L343@P12: marie to reheat food. Bain marie is not designed for reheating food. If attempted, the food typically
L344@P12: spends many hours at a perfect temperature for bacterial growth. Food must be freshly cooked or
L345@P12: reheated prior to display in a bain marie.
L346@P12: If the business is worried that food may lose quality at the temperature of greater than 60°C, the
L347@P12: business must use time as a control (such as the 2-hour/4-hour rule). Records of the times and
L348@P12: temperatures that food is displayed must be kept to ensure that the food is safe.
L349@P12: Salads and cut meats displayed above 5°C
L350@P12: It is common for salad and sandwich bars to display cold food at 5 to 10°C and it can be difficult to rectify
L351@P12: the issue without significant investment. Brightly lit displays, with “cold plate” refrigeration, stacked high
L352@P12: with food to be more appetising, are more difficult to manage than compact displays with overhead
L353@P12: cooling.
L354@P12: Sometimes the problem can be traced to operator actions, such as placing spacers (cardboard, foam or
L355@P12: bread) between the cold plate and the food tray to avoid freezing. In this case the use of plastic trays
L356@P12: might be a better option.
L357@P12: Often the only solution available to operators is to display suitable quantities of food and to remove food
L358@P12: from display consistent with the “2-hour/4-hour rule”. Condiments, sauces and other non PHF do not
L359@P12: have to follow the rule.
L360@P12: Pre-cut sandwiches and rolls
L361@P12: Sandwich shops sometimes prepare sandwiches ahead of the lunch rush hour. Sandwiches are a
L362@P12: potentially hazardous food, and time or temperature control is necessary. If adequate refrigeration is not
L363@P12-13: available, then sandwiches must be sold within the 4-hour time frame and time control must be evident.Potentially hazardous foods – Foods that require temperature control for safety

L365@P13: Ambient or cool display of sushi
L366@P13: Sushi businesses are reluctant to refrigerate their products because changes to rice starch can result in
L367@P13: a firmer texture and starchy taste. Even though the rice is acidified, some components of the sushi

L370@P13: no more than 4 hours provided:

L373@P13: • Proper acidification of the rice (to a pH less than or equal to 4.6) takes place to inhibit the growth of

L379@P13: Ambient display of seemingly perishable Asian foods
L380@P13: Anecdotally, Asian communities have a tradition of “cook–serve” food service and equate refrigerated
L381@P13: products to old or stale food. However, many Asian specialties “cook–serve” has been replaced by a
L382@P13: longer supply chain that includes centralised processing, overnight (or early morning) delivery and
L383@P13: extended display times often with no temperature control. Even when foods are prepared at the retail
L384@P13: premises, if temperature and time controls are lacking, it is not possible to comply with the Food
L385@P13: Standards Code requirements for display of potentially hazardous foods.
L386@P13: The wide variety of often unfamiliar foods might present a challenge for Authorised Officers. Most
L387@P13: products will be exactly as they seem — potentially hazardous. A small number of foods appear
L388@P13: potentially hazardous but previous studies have revealed that they are low risk foods.
