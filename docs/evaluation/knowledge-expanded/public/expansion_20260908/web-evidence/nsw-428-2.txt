L334@P12: Verifying that foods are not potentially hazardous
L335@P12: Food businesses are responsible to determine which foods they serve, or sell are potentially hazardous.
L336@P12: Authorised officers require evidence of the determination, for example (but not limited to) a validation
L337@P12: study, analytical testing results or the result of a challenge testing.
L338@P12: Food businesses are also required to substantiate food safety controls when handling PHF.
L339@P12: Common issues
L340@P12: Warm food (less than 60°C) in a bain marie
L341@P12: A correctly adjusted bain marie can hold food at 60°to 65°C without generating too much steam. If food
L342@P12: on display is less than 60°C, the unit could be set too low, or the operator might have used the bain
L343@P12: marie to reheat food. Bain marie is not designed for reheating food. If attempted, the food typically
L344@P12: spends many hours at a perfect temperature for bacterial growth. Food must be freshly cooked or
L345@P12: reheated prior to display in a bain marie.
L346@P12: If the business is worried that food may lose quality at the temperature of greater than 60°C, the
L347@P12: business must use time as a control (such as the 2-hour/4-hour rule). Records of the times and
L348@P12: temperatures that food is displayed must be kept to ensure that the food is safe.
L349@P12: Salads and cut meats displayed above 5°C
L350@P12: It is common for salad and sandwich bars to display cold food at 5 to 10°C and it can be difficult to rectify
L351@P12: the issue without significant investment. Brightly lit displays, with “cold plate” refrigeration, stacked high
L352@P12: with food to be more appetising, are more difficult to manage than compact displays with overhead
L353@P12: cooling.
L354@P12: Sometimes the problem can be traced to operator actions, such as placing spacers (cardboard, foam or
L355@P12: bread) between the cold plate and the food tray to avoid freezing. In this case the use of plastic trays
L356@P12: might be a better option.
L357@P12: Often the only solution available to operators is to display suitable quantities of food and to remove food
L358@P12: from display consistent with the “2-hour/4-hour rule”. Condiments, sauces and other non PHF do not
L359@P12: have to follow the rule.
L360@P12: Pre-cut sandwiches and rolls
L361@P12: Sandwich shops sometimes prepare sandwiches ahead of the lunch rush hour. Sandwiches are a
L362@P12: potentially hazardous food, and time or temperature control is necessary. If adequate refrigeration is not
L363@P12-13: available, then sandwiches must be sold within the 4-hour time frame and time control must be evident.Potentially hazardous foods – Foods that require temperature control for safety

L365@P13: Ambient or cool display of sushi
L366@P13: Sushi businesses are reluctant to refrigerate their products because changes to rice starch can result in
L367@P13: a firmer texture and starchy taste. Even though the rice is acidified, some components of the sushi
L368@P13: remain potentially hazardous, which causes the RTE sushi to be categorised as PHF.
L369@P13: The NSW Food Authority permits retail sushi businesses to display sushi at temperatures up to 25°C for
L370@P13: no more than 4 hours provided:
L371@P13: • The Food Standards Code’s requirements are followed during the receipt, preparation and storage
L372@P13: of sushi and raw materials;
L373@P13: • Proper acidification of the rice (to a pH less than or equal to 4.6) takes place to inhibit the growth of
L374@P13: food poisoning bacteria. The pH must be recorded; and
L375@P13: • Sushi businesses implement a system for monitoring the length of time sushi is displayed at 5oC to
L376@P13: 25oC.
L377@P13: Further information can be found in the ‘Food safety guidelines for the preparation and display of sushi’
L378@P13: on the Food Authority’s website www.foodauthority.nsw.gov.au/resource-centre.
L379@P13: Ambient display of seemingly perishable Asian foods
L380@P13: Anecdotally, Asian communities have a tradition of “cook–serve” food service and equate refrigerated
L381@P13: products to old or stale food. However, many Asian specialties “cook–serve” has been replaced by a
L382@P13: longer supply chain that includes centralised processing, overnight (or early morning) delivery and
L383@P13: extended display times often with no temperature control. Even when foods are prepared at the retail
L384@P13: premises, if temperature and time controls are lacking, it is not possible to comply with the Food
L385@P13: Standards Code requirements for display of potentially hazardous foods.
L386@P13: The wide variety of often unfamiliar foods might present a challenge for Authorised Officers. Most
L387@P13: products will be exactly as they seem — potentially hazardous. A small number of foods appear
L388@P13: potentially hazardous but previous studies have revealed that they are low risk foods.
L389@P13: Chinese-style roast meats (such as roast duck, roast chicken, roast pork, BBQ
L390@P13: pork)
L391@P13: Despite the concerns about lack of temperature control, Chinese-style roast meats have rarely been
L392@P13: implicated in foodborne illness incidents. The conventional cooking method for these products produce
L393@P13: internal temperatures high enough to destroy all vegetative pathogens resulting in the absence or very
L394@P13: low levels of microbial load on the products immediately after cooking. The products are then promptly
L395@P13: moved to the display cabinet, away from the main kitchen, which is vital in avoiding cross-contamination
L396@P13: between the cooked and raw meat (Ying, 2000).
L397@P13: NSW Health previously reviewed these products and established a tolerance for BBQ shop practices.
L398@P13: The Department of Health Victoria also studied Chinese roast meats and established specific food safety
L399@P13: plans for the products. A study in Canada (Lao, Sidhu & Shaw, 2014) also found roast pork to have
L400@P13: water activity much lower than 0.85 (0.7 for the skin and 0.81 for the cavity).
L401@P13: The NSW Food Authority’s position is that traditionally prepared BBQ pork, duck and chicken on the day
L402@P13: of preparation are low risk products until they are cut up for sale. After cutting, the protection provided by
L403@P13: scalding, surface drying and roasting in a salty-sugary glaze is lost and the products become perishable
L404@P13: and potentially hazardous. The products then must follow the “2-hour/4-hour rule”.
L405@P13: The Department of Health Victoria’s document includes additional information on other BBQ shop
L406@P13: products. It is important to note that the “low risk” status does not extend to the miscellaneous products
L407@P13-14: sold by the BBQ shops such as gizzard, squid and tongue.Potentially hazardous foods – Foods that require temperature control for safety

L409@P14: Some BBQ shops prepare large batches of roast meats early in the day and sell them until the shop
L410@P14: closes. Other shops prepare smaller batches several times throughout the day. The latter practice is
L411@P14: recommended by the NSW Food Authority.
L412@P14: Asian cakes and desserts
L413@P14: A number of products look potentially hazardous and like many other cakes, under the IFT/FDA model
L414@P14: they are in the query zone. However, the high sugar content and the processing steps may make them
L415@P14: not potentially hazardous.
L416@P14: The Department of Health Victoria published FoodAtlas (www.foodatlas.health.vic.gov.au) which
L417@P14: provides information about the physical, chemical and compositional properties, their associated food
L418@P14: safety risk and the processes they undergo for culturally diverse foods.
L419@P14: In 2017, the NSW Food Authority conducted a survey of rice-based desserts, commonly known as rice
L420@P14: cakes and found that steam cooking was effective in destroying any vegetative microorganisms and
L421@P14: these products should be safe to be stored at room temperature until the end of the next day (day of
L422@P14: manufacturing +1). These products are more likely to spoil before they become unsafe. Further
L423@P14: information can be found in ‘Rice based desserts’ document on the Food Authority’s website
L424@P14: www.foodauthority.nsw.gov.au/resource-centre.
L425@P14: Asian fresh noodles and rice rolls
L426@P14: Some types of fresh, Asian-style noodles are potentially hazardous because they have a high-water
L427@P14: activity level and a near neutral pH. Contaminated or spoiled products may not look deteriorated, so they
L428@P14: must be refrigerated at all times to minimise the risk to consumers.
L429@P14: Retailers sometimes reluctant to put these products in the refrigerator because they harden when
L430@P14: refrigerated and some customers may think that the noodles are not fresh. The ‘use-by’ or ‘best-before’
L431@P14: date, production date (if it is included), and storage and cooking instructions on the packaging should
L432@P14: reassure consumers that the products are fresh. Noodles will soften once they are reheated. The
L433@P14: packaging should provide instructions on how to do this.
L434@P14: Note that some types of fresh noodles have been specifically treated and vacuum packaged so they can
L435@P14: be stored on the shelf.
L436@P14: Baked goods
L437@P14: Products with fresh cream or custard filled bakery products generally have pH and aw which causes
L438@P14: them to be categorised as potentially hazardous. As a result, they need the temperature or time control
L439@P14: for safety. This is supported by a history of food poisoning (caused primarily by Salmonella and less
L440@P14: frequently S. aureus and Bacillus cereus) attributed to these products.
L441@P14: Mock cream filled products, tarts, cookies, plain cakes, and muffins are typically stored at ambient
L442@P14: temperature on the day for which they are manufactured. The products have a relatively low aw, a much
L443@P14: drier protective crust and a long-established history of safe ambient storage.
L444@P14: The pH or aw of bread products are generally not low enough to classify the products as not potentially
L445@P14: hazardous. However, other characteristics such as the dry protective crust mean that plain bread does
L446@P14: not require refrigeration for food safety reasons. The same reason applies to bread rolls with baked-on
L447@P14: toppings. However, products that have recently been introduced to the market with readily perishable
L448@P14: fillings or fillings/toppings added after baking have been classified as potentially hazardous. They are
L449@P14-15: generally displayed in display cases under time control (the 2-hour/4-hour rule).Potentially hazardous foods – Foods that require temperature control for safety

L451@P15: Alternative Methods of Compliance
L452@P15: Clause 25 of Food Standard 3.2.2 outlines how food businesses can demonstrate that an alternative
L453@P15: system of time or temperature control will not adversely affect the microbiological safety of the food.
L454@P15: Food Regulation 2015 establishes how the use of the word “demonstrate” in the Food Standards Code is
L455@P15: to be interpreted:
L456@P15: Part 6 Clause 38 (1) (d) ‘to demonstrate is to be read as a reference to demonstrate to the
L457@P15: satisfaction of the Food Authority’.
L458@P15: Demonstrating alternative compliance
L459@P15: Companies may demonstrate alternative compliance by having validated food safety plans. However,
L460@P15: food safety plans are not routinely used in the food sectors where the common issues (identified above)
L461@P15: are encountered. If a food safety program is used to demonstrate compliance it must identify the risks
L462@P15: inherent in food display. If the food is potentially hazardous then the controls employed for safe display
L463@P15: must be validated and systematically monitored. This approach has been used by the Victorian
L464@P15: Department of Health for Chinese roast meats.
L465@P15: Companies may also demonstrate compliance based on sound scientific evidence or written guidelines
L466@P15: published by the relevant food industry. In both cases there must be documented evidence of the
L467@P15: completion of a suitable study. This will often be a challenge study, which is discussed in the previous
L468@P15: section, but could also be based on microbiological growth models or an expert report.
L469@P15: For more information on alternative compliance, please refer to the ‘Developing Innovative Food Safety
L470@P15-16: Control Measures’ document on the Food Authority’s website.Potentially hazardous foods – Foods that require temperature control for safety

L472@P16: Conclusion
L473@P16: There are a number of important questions that a business must answer when deciding if temperature or
L474@P16: time control is required for food on display:
L475@P16: 1. Could the food contain a pathogen that can cause foodborne illness if it multiplies? With a few
L476@P16: exceptions, such as commercially canned foods, the answer to this question is “yes”. Cooked
L477@P16: foods can have residual contamination in the form of heat resistant spores. Foods handled after
L478@P16: cooking are exposed to further contamination by pathogenic bacteria.
L479@P16: 2. Will the food support the growth of pathogenic bacteria? The tables and charts on page 11 and in
L480@P16: the Appendices provide guidance on this question. All or parts of most meals will readily support
L481@P16: pathogen growth and their safety is dependent on temperature or time-controlled display.
L482@P16: 3. Is the food too dry to support pathogen growth? The answer is “yes” for a few ready-to-eat foods
L483@P16: such as jerky and fruit cake. For most foods the answer is “no”, and pathogen growth can occur.
L484@P16: A laboratory test for water activity can provide an answer but the most perishable components of
L485@P16: the food must be tested.
L486@P16: 4. Is the food too acidic to support pathogen growth? For some products such as pickles, some
L487@P16: sauces and fruit juices the answer is “yes”. For most ready-to-eat foods the answer is “no”. A
L488@P16: laboratory test for pH can provide an answer but again the most perishable components must be
L489@P16: tested.
L490@P16: 5. Are there other factors that might make the food safe?
L491@P16: The final question leads businesses into food technology, expert advice and laboratory testing. If there
L492@P16: are sound technical reasons that make temperature or time control unworkable, then the business might
L493@P16: have no choice other than to seek an answer to this question.
L494@P16: This protocol is a guide and does not replace good science or good judgment applied by the business to
L495@P16: their product and process. Furthermore, this protocol does not limit the ability of the business to develop
L496@P16: additional data beyond those described in this protocol to demonstrate the safety of their food product.
L497@P16: The NSW Food Authority considers that any food product that fails to meet the definition of “not
L498@P16-17: potentially hazardous” is subject to the temperature control requirements of the Food Standards Code.Potentially hazardous foods – Foods that require temperature control for safety
